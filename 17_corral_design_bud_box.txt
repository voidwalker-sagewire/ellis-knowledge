PART 1: PASTURE AND CORRAL HANDLING BASICS (NRCS/Bud Williams/Grandin methods)

IN THE PASTURE

- Remember flight zones: use just enough pressure to move an animal without sending it
  over the fence.
- Point of balance: livestock tend to move FORWARD if you're behind their shoulder and
  BACKWARD if you're in front of their shoulder. If livestock won't move forward in an
  alleyway, try walking past them from front to back (with a hotshot set down, not in
  use).
- Take your time: it's faster to do it right the first time than to redo it.
- Keep moving: people in motion are easy for livestock to read and relate to. When
  motion stops, livestock get nervous - their instinct is to turn and clearly see what
  stopped, because in nature something that stops suddenly might be a predator about to
  pounce. Move back and forth BEHIND a group rather than following straight behind them.
- Walk in straight lines, not curves.
- Guide the FRONT animals, not the middle of the herd - the majority will follow the
  leaders.
- Don't stop when you reach an open gate. Livestock already know the gate is there -
  push them through at a reasonable pace. Stopping to "let them find the hole" removes
  all pressure, causing them to turn and face you, which means starting the whole
  approach over again. Going straight through is simpler.
- Cull animals that are consistently wild or aggressive - this is a genetics/temperament
  issue that gentle handling alone often can't fix, and it protects both the handler and
  the rest of the herd's calm.

IN THE CORRAL

- Eliminate shadows, trash, chains, and other visual distractions - these cause balking.
- Don't yell.
- Remove protruding bolts, nails, sharp corners - safety for both livestock and people.
- Eliminate noisy chutes/headgates where possible.
- Always have an escape route in mind for yourself.
- Don't fight an animal that gets mad - one of you will get hurt, and it doesn't matter
  if "the animal wins" that particular moment.
- Livestock like to go back where they came from - USE this instinct when designing
  facilities and moving animals into crowd tubs for sorting, rather than fighting it.
- Don't overfill the crowd tub and lanes.
- Don't place a back-up gate right at the entrance of the lane from the crowd tub - it
  causes balking. Place it one body-length up from the entrance instead.
- On hotshot (electric prod) use: think of it as if the hotshot had a short in the
  handle, so every time you shock an animal, you get shocked too. Use it only when
  absolutely necessary - this framing naturally cuts down unnecessary use. Often an
  animal would have moved on its own if you'd waited just two more seconds before
  reaching for it.
- Take your time in the corral too - there are no medals for the fastest handling job,
  and rushing usually costs more time than it saves through balking and re-work.
- Before handling livestock, discuss the plan and goals with your crew - a shared plan
  prevents conflicting pressure/positioning that confuses the herd.

PART 2: THE BUD BOX DESIGN (K-State Extension, MF3349)

WHAT IT IS

Developed by stockmanship instructor Bud Williams. Essentially a rectangular pen where
the alleyway leading to the chute sits at a RIGHT ANGLE to the point of entry into the
box. Cattle are brought in, and the handler's position along the opposite side
stimulates them to move past the handler and into the alleyway. A gate closes behind
the cattle as they enter, forming the box; the handler then works along the fence
adjacent to the alley, moving forward toward the closed end to push cattle past
themselves and into the alley.

WHY IT WORKS - THREE BEHAVIOR PRINCIPLES
1. Cattle want to move past the handler
2. Cattle want to be with other cattle (herd instinct)
3. Cattle want to go back the way they came in

IMPORTANT USAGE RULE: A Bud Box should NOT be used as a holding pen. Only bring in as
many cattle as will fit down the alley to the chute at one time - overfilling defeats
the design's psychology.

DESIGN CHARACTERISTICS VS. A CONVENTIONAL ROUND TUB

Round/circular tub systems use large sweeping alleyways and multiple crowding gates -
more material, larger footprint, higher construction cost. A Bud Box has OPEN SIDES,
no sweeping bends, and doesn't need multiple crowding gates - cheaper to build and a
smaller footprint. It typically does NOT need solid sheeting/sides (open sides support
natural cattle movement and visibility of the handler); sheeting can be added only
where specific distractions are impairing flow.

BASIC DIMENSIONS
Standard Bud Box: 12 feet wide x 20 feet long (deep), closed on one end with a gate at
the other; alleyway placed perpendicular to the box at the gated end.
Minimum box (12'x20') handles processing of 5-6 head of mature cows at a time. General
rule: 5-6 feet of alleyway length per head of mature cattle. Alleyways should be a
minimum of 28 inches wide.
Curved alley sections (if used) should be long enough to fit at least 2 head, so
following animals can always see the animal ahead - visibility of a herd-mate ahead
keeps cattle moving calmly through a curve.
Loadout boxes for semi-trailers should be sized larger - a common size is a 14-foot-wide
alley, 30 feet deep, to accommodate the larger head counts filling trailer
compartments.
Handler-on-foot alleys: minimum recommended width 12 feet. If horses are used to move
cattle through the facility: recommended alley width increases to 16 feet.

ADAPTABILITY

The Bud Box concept adapts to many pen configurations: split/double alleyways for
additional sorting or loading capacity, curved alleys where space requires it (larger
diameter curves work better - a lead animal stays visible to followers; too small a
diameter leaves insufficient room to hold 4-5 head in the single-file section), round
tubs converted to Bud-Box-style exit logic (cattle enter, turn, and exit through a
single-file alley NEAR the entrance point rather than 90-180 degrees away, as is
typical with conventional half/quarter-circle tubs), straight pens, angled pens
(reduces 90-degree turns to improve flow), integration into an existing building,
pie-shaped multi-purpose pens (useful for smaller operations needing flexible feeding/
holding/sick pens, with perimeter space for feed bunks/water troughs), and placement
at the corner of multiple adjoining pastures for a shared working facility.

Additional practical features that can be incorporated: emergency fence passes
(allowing a handler to step through the fence to escape if needed), rear stop gates
inside alleyways, and dedicated calving-assistance setups (headgate + swing-away side
gates) when integrated into a building.

IMPORTANT CAVEAT FROM THE SOURCE

The Bud Box "requires a basic understanding of cattle handling principles and may
require additional training for inexperienced individuals. As with any cattle working
facility, the cattle-handling skills and attitude of the handler influence the success
of the facility." In other words: the facility design supports good stockmanship, but
doesn't replace it - a Bud Box used by a handler who doesn't understand flight
zone/point of balance principles won't perform as intended.
