SITE CONSIDERATIONS FOR REVEGETATION

Revegetation is expensive (100+ dollars/acre at time of writing) and not guaranteed to
succeed - evaluate first whether the pasture can instead be RESTORED (deferred grazing,
weed control, rotational grazing with proper stocking) rather than fully reseeded.

Soil texture drives species selection: loamy soils are the best all-around medium.
Sandy soils are hard to establish on but, once established, PRODUCE MORE forage than
any other dryland site type. Clay soils pack hard and need mechanical loosening before
seeding.

Saline soils (electrical conductivity/EC of 4.0 mmhos/cm or greater): EC of 12+ limits
species to only a few tolerant options (tall wheatgrass, pubescent wheatgrass, alkali
sacaton, Russian wildrye). No forage grows above EC 30.

Precipitation drives species options dramatically: a difference of just 2 inches of
average annual precipitation is enough to change which species will survive best.
Sites at 12" or less: limited to Russian wildrye and certain crested wheatgrass
varieties. Species options increase substantially above 14"/year.

BUNCHGRASSES VS SOD-FORMERS

Bunchgrasses grow in clumps/bunches (e.g. crested wheatgrass, orchardgrass). Typically
produce MORE forage but need more intensive grazing management - growth of some
bunchgrasses is set back when developing seed stalks are grazed. Some (orchardgrass,
perennial ryegrass, meadow brome) continually initiate new tillers and are less
impacted by defoliation.

Sod-formers spread via underground rhizomes that tie soil together (e.g. Kentucky
bluegrass, western wheatgrass, smooth brome). Typically less productive but MORE
grazing-tolerant since they resprout from rhizomes even after heavy grazing.

WARM VS COOL SEASON GRASSES

Cool-season grasses (e.g. orchardgrass) grow best in cool, moist spring months, regrow
in fall with moisture, typically go dormant in summer heat.

Warm-season grasses (e.g. blue grama, bluestems) don't begin growth until soil hits
~60°F (mid-May), adapted to summer heat, go dormant in fall.

MIXING WARM AND COOL SEASON GRASSES: not advised in the same pasture - cool-season
grasses get first use of spring moisture/nutrients and tend to dominate over time,
especially if spring-overgrazed. Better to establish as SEPARATE pastures and use each
seasonally (cool-season pasture in spring/fall, warm-season in summer) - though this
may not be practical on small acreages.

NATIVE VS INTRODUCED GRASSES

Native grasses: evolved with local climate/soil, live longer, need little maintenance,
BUT more expensive seed and need 3-4 years to establish before grazing.

Introduced grasses: establish faster (grazeable in 1-3 years, except Russian wildrye
which needs up to 4), respond better to fertilizer/irrigation, cheaper seed, but may
lack the longevity of natives.

Do NOT mix native and introduced grasses in the same seeding - introduced species will
out-compete natives. Mixtures of native species together interact well and are
recommended.

LEGUMES IN PASTURE MIXES

Legumes host Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules that convert atmospheric nitrogen into
plant-available form, sharing some with neighboring grasses over time. Mixing legumes
and grasses can increase TOTAL forage production by up to 30% (dependent on adequate
soil moisture). Alfalfa fixes the most nitrogen; clovers and others fix somewhat less.
Legumes also extend the growing season (make more summer growth than cool-season
grasses) and generally improve palatability, digestibility, and nutrient content.

BLOAT WARNING: Keep bloat-causing legumes (alfalfa, clovers) to 25% or LESS of a
grazed grass-legume stand. These cause gas buildup trapped in the rumen that can lead
to rapid death. Non-bloating legume alternatives include cicer milkvetch, sainfoin,
and birdsfoot trefoil.

SEED MIXTURES VS MONOCULTURE

Mixtures: more ecologically resilient (disease/insect/climate extremes), more efficient
users of moisture/light/nutrients across the season, better weed competition. Simple
3-4 species mixes for irrigated pastures; more species common for dryland. Select
species similar in palatability, regrowth ability, maturity timing, and competitiveness
- livestock will overgraze the most palatable species in an unbalanced mix.

Some species are better managed as MONOCULTURES: crested wheatgrass, Russian wildrye
(both provide forage 2-3 weeks earlier than others - easier to manage without
competition), pubescent wheatgrass, smooth brome, and tall fescue.

SEEDING PRACTICALITIES

Pure Live Seed (PLS) = seed purity x seed germination (as decimals). Always buy/plant
based on PLS, not raw bulk weight, to account for dead seed, weed seed, and inert
matter in the bag.
Formula: (PLS lbs/acre desired) / (PLS decimal) = bulk lbs/acre to buy.

Seeding window: dormant seeding Nov 15-April 15 is generally best for dryland (soil not
frozen, ready to grow with spring rain). With irrigation, forage can be seeded 6-8 weeks
before first hard freeze (roughly late Aug-early Sept).

Newly established dryland grasses should NOT be grazed until after they set seed - this
takes 2-4 years for natives, at least 1 year for introduced species on dryland.
Irrigated grasses can be lightly grazed in the seeding year once 4+ leaves and 1-2
tillers are showing.

GRAZING MANAGEMENT - CORE CONCEPTS

Overgrazing is the #1 cause of pasture decline. It happens when (a) plants aren't given
adequate regrowth time, or (b) grazing pressure isn't reduced during drought. Forages
need enough LEAF AREA remaining after grazing to capture sunlight and rebuild root/stem
carbohydrate reserves.

Recovery period needed varies: dryland pastures typically need 30-90 days; irrigated
land 15-30 days. Cool-season grasses regrow best in cool weather; warm-season in hot
weather - both need adequate soil moisture regardless.

DROUGHT RULE: when precipitation is 75% of average or lower for several months,
drought conditions exist. Grazing management should become MORE conservative (fewer
animals, shorter periods) during BOTH the drought itself and the growing season that
follows it - overgrazing during drought greatly increases grass mortality risk.

MINIMUM STUBBLE HEIGHTS (leave this much after grazing, general guide)
- Most cool-season grasses: 4 inches
- Crested/western wheatgrass: 3 inches
- Tall wheatgrass: 6 inches
- Tall warm-season species: 6 inches
- Little bluestem, sideoats grama: 4 inches
- Blue grama: 2 inches
- Most irrigated grasses: 4 inches; Kentucky bluegrass: 2 inches
- Alfalfa: 3-4 inches; Birdsfoot trefoil: 4-6 inches; Sainfoin: 3-4 inches
- White clover: 2 inches; Red/alsike clover: 4 inches
If pastures are grazed CONTINUOUSLY (no 30+ day rest periods), DOUBLE these stubble
heights to compensate.

REGRAZING RULE OF THUMB: allow plants to regrow to roughly TWICE their minimum stubble
height before allowing regrazing.

A PROPERLY grazed pasture looks UNEVEN - most plants grazed to minimum stubble height,
some shorter, some taller/ungrazed patches (normal, from trampling/dung/urine
avoidance or natural grazing patterns). If a pasture looks uniformly mowed-short
across the board, it's overgrazed.

STOCKING RATE AND CARRYING CAPACITY CALCULATIONS

1 Animal Unit (AU) = 1,000 lb cow (or five 200-lb sheep). Adjust proportionally for
actual animal weight (e.g. 1,300 lb cow = 1.3 AU). 1 AU eats ~25 lbs air-dry forage/day
(a 1,000-lb horse eats/tramples more - equivalent to 1.25 AU, ~31 lbs/day).

Realistic utilization: even a properly-grazed pasture only yields about 25% of total
annual forage production to actual livestock consumption - roughly 50% must remain as
stubble for plant health, and another 25% is lost to trampling, insects, rodents, and
fouling.

Carrying capacity formula:
Total forage (lbs) = yield per acre x acres
Grazeable forage = Total forage x 0.25
Grazing days = Grazeable forage / (25 lbs/day per AU, or 31 for a horse)

WORKED EXAMPLE: 30-acre pasture producing 1,000 lbs/acre/year forage, grazed by one
1,000-lb horse (1.25 AU):
- 1,000 x 30 = 30,000 lbs total forage
- 30,000 x 0.25 = 7,500 lbs grazeable forage
- 7,500 / 31 lbs/day = 242 days of grazing for one horse (or 121 days for two horses)
- Carrying capacity = 242 days / 30 acres = ~8 horse-days per acre per year

GENERAL PASTURE MANAGEMENT GUIDELINES (checklist)

1. Know your pasture's carrying capacity and don't exceed it without justification
   from actual measured forage growth.
2. If animal numbers exceed carrying capacity, sell/remove excess or feed supplementally.
3. Overgrazing happens two ways: leaving animals too long, OR not allowing enough
   regrowth time between grazings.
4. Monitor minimum stubble heights and overall pasture appearance; move animals when
   proper use is reached.
5. Avoid grazing wet pastures (soil compaction, plant crown damage, trampling risk).
6. Spring is critical - forages are drawing on root reserves for new growth. Delay
   grazing until plants reach 5-6 inches (3-5 leaves for grasses).
7. Vary WHEN plants are grazed each year (don't always graze during the sensitive
   "boot stage" when seed heads are elevating) to protect long-term plant health.
8. Rotational systems need a MINIMUM of 4 paddocks (2 is the absolute floor) - typical
   rest periods run 30-90 days depending on season/moisture.
9. Restrict animal access to rivers/creeks/ponds to protect water quality.

FOR UNGRAZED PASTURES: mow every other year to recycle nutrients and let light reach
new leaf growth (skip mowing during drought). Monitor yearly for noxious weeds and
control before seed set.

SPECIES CHARACTERISTICS QUICK REFERENCE (selected species, condensed from Appendix B/C)

GRASSES (habit / season / origin / min. moisture / soil / notes):
- Kentucky bluegrass: sod, cool, introduced, needs irrigation, silt-clay soils, low
  yield potential but excellent longevity, good grazing/trampling tolerance
- Big bluestem: bunch, warm, native, 14"+ min moisture, sand-silt-clay, high yield,
  excellent longevity/drought tolerance, fair grazing tolerance (grazed out if
  mismanaged)
- Little bluestem: bunch, warm, native, 14"+, high yield, excellent drought tolerance
- Smooth brome: sod, cool, introduced, 16"+, medium yield, good longevity, fair
  drought tolerance, fair-poor recovery after grazing (regrows slowly, use lightly)
- Meadow brome: bunch, cool, introduced, needs irrigation, medium yield, good recovery
  after cutting/grazing, more winter-hardy than orchardgrass
- Tall fescue: bunch, cool, introduced, needs irrigation, high yield, good longevity,
  good grazing tolerance, good recovery. CAUTION: often infected with an endophyte
  fungus producing alkaloid toxins causing "fescue foot" and toxicosis in livestock -
  seek ENDOPHYTE-FREE or "novel/friendly endophyte" varieties (e.g. those marketed as
  MaxQ-type) which retain stress tolerance without the toxin. Pregnant/lactating mares
  should not consume infected tall fescue.
- Blue grama: sod, warm, native, 10"+, low yield but excellent drought tolerance and
  grazing tolerance - very resilient, don't graze below 2"
- Sideoats grama: bunch, warm, native, 12"+, medium yield, good drought tolerance,
  fair grazing tolerance
- Orchardgrass: bunch, cool, introduced, needs irrigation, medium yield, excellent
  recovery rate after grazing/cutting, but low tolerance to trampling and doesn't
  tolerate close/continuous grazing
- Switchgrass: sod, warm, native, 14-16"+, high yield, good drought tolerance, good
  grazing tolerance
- Crested wheatgrass: bunch, cool, introduced from Russia, 8-12"+, medium yield,
  excellent grazing tolerance, provides forage 2-3 weeks EARLIER than most grasses but
  goes dormant/poor quality by June-August
- Western wheatgrass: sod, cool, native, 12"+, good yield, excellent salt tolerance,
  good drought/grazing tolerance
- Russian wildrye: bunch, cool, introduced, 8"+, excellent drought tolerance and
  grazing tolerance, difficult to establish, poor for hay (mostly basal leaves) but
  excellent dryland pasture - good strategy is light spring grazing, saving regrowth
  for late summer/fall when other grasses are unavailable

LEGUMES:
- Alfalfa ("Queen of Forages"): highly productive, deep taproot (10-30 ft), widely
  adapted but needs well-drained soil, CAUSES BLOAT, needs 30-day+ rest periods to
  persist under grazing, don't graze below 4-6"
- Red clover: short-lived (2-4 years), self-reseeds, more tolerant of acidic/poorly
  drained soil than alfalfa, similar bloat risk, high production
- White clover (including Ladino type): shallow-rooted, spreads via stolons, needs
  moisture, tolerant of summer heat, causes bloat if over 50% of stand, benefits from
  frequent spring grazing of companion cool-season grasses
- Cicer milkvetch: very long-lived, creeping rhizomes, does NOT cause bloat, tolerant
  of alkaline soil (up to pH 9.8), slow to establish (2-3 years) but can become
  dominant (up to 80% of a stand) over time, less palatable than other legumes so needs
  heavier grazing pressure to keep it in check
- Sainfoin: does NOT cause bloat, deep-rooted, heat/drought/cold resistant, difficult
  to establish, grows ahead of alfalfa in spring for early high-protein grazing, only
  ~3 year stand life
- Birdsfoot trefoil: does NOT cause bloat, long-lived, performs well on poorly-drained
  clay soils and low-fertility/alkaline soils where alfalfa struggles, highly palatable,
  withstands grazing better than most legumes, needs taller stubble left (regrowth
  comes from stem buds, not crown) and 4-6 week rest between grazings

GENERAL SITE-MATCHING PRINCIPLE: match species to soil texture (sand/silt/clay),
moisture availability, and salinity BEFORE anything else - the guide includes a full
site-by-site species suggestion table (clay/loam, sandy, saline, gravelly foothills,
subirrigated, irrigated-adequate-water, irrigated-short-water, low-maintenance) that a
local NRCS or extension office can help match to a specific property.
