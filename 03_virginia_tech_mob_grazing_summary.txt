INTRODUCTION

This paper summarizes several research studies that addressed mob-type grazing in
Virginia, conducted 2012-2016, comparing three grazing systems: mob, rotational, and
continuous grazing. Measured: forage productivity, forage nutritional value, animal
performance, clover populations, and soil health indices.

MOB VS ROTATIONAL GRAZING - DEFINITIONS

Mob grazing involves intensive decision-making to control livestock stocking rates and
forage removal. First promoted by Allan Savory in the 1980s as part of holistic rangeland
management, later adopted in eastern pasturelands (Joel Salatin). A large number of
animals are restricted to a small area, eating or trampling all plants before moving to new
grass - sometimes after just a few hours. Grazing usually starts later in the season
(late May/June in Virginia) when pastures have more growth, followed by a long recovery
period of 90+ days. Mob-grazed pastures may be grazed only once or twice per season.

Rotational grazing uses recurring periods of grazing and rest among three or more
paddocks - similar in principle to mob grazing except stocking density is lower and
recovery periods much shorter (15-30 days).

Continuous grazing = minimal management of stocking rate or forage removal control.

CLAIMED BENEFITS OF MOB/ROTATIONAL METHODS (from prior literature)

1. Healthy soil with high organic matter, water-holding capacity, and abundance of
   microorganisms, earthworms, and dung beetles.
2. Even distribution of recycled soil nutrients and organic matter from intensive
   management of animal stocking density.
3. Desirable plant diversity with few weeds and consistent seasonal ground cover,
   building organic matter and reducing soil erosion.

STUDY SITES

Three locations: two demonstration farms (Blacksburg and Raphine, VA, 2013-2015) and
Virginia Tech's Shenandoah Valley Agriculture Research and Experiment Center (SVAREC)
in Steeles Tavern, VA (2014-2016, replicated 3x). Soils predominately silt loams;
vegetation dominated by tall fescue, orchardgrass, and Kentucky bluegrass.

GRAZING PROTOCOLS

At Raphine/Blacksburg: mob stocking = two 12-16hr stocking periods/year, stocking
densities 125,000-140,000 lbs live weight/acre on 0.25-0.5 acre paddocks, 90-120 day
rest periods. Rotational = 6-7 stocking periods of 3-4 days on 0.75-2 acre paddocks with
fixed 28-30 day rest periods.

At SVAREC: mob = three stocking periods/year on 0.1 ha paddocks reallocated every 24hr,
not back-fenced, ~40,000 lbs/acre stocking density, 64-day rest period.

RESULTS

Forage production: Mob-grazed paddocks accumulated ~540 lbs/acre MORE standing forage
than rotational or continuous - but this was because cattle ate LESS of it, due to trampling
making much of the grass difficult to graze. Forage accumulation rates didn't differ between
methods, but disappearance (actual use by cattle) was lower under mob stocking.

Nutritional value: Continuous pastures generally had higher crude protein, mainly due
to higher white clover content (cattle preferentially select clover for its palatability/protein).
Nutritive values under mob grazing were reasonable for dry cows but trended toward the
lower threshold for maintenance by 2015, since grasses were allowed to become excessively
over-mature each year before grazing.

Clover establishment: Continuous pastures had more white clover (shorter grazing favors
white clover establishment). Red clover established relatively well under ALL systems -
its upright growth habit reduced shading by grasses under mob/rotational stocking.
Contrary to hypothesis, mob grazing did not meaningfully suppress clover establishment.

Soil organic matter (SOM): Averaged 3-3.5% at both demonstration sites. No substantial
difference in SOM increase between mob/rotational vs. continuous grazing over the
3-year study period. Soil carbon pools were more strongly affected by site than by
grazing system - likely because these soils had been established pasture for years and
were near carbon saturation in surface layers already. Researchers speculate 5-10 years
may be needed to see significant soil changes from grazing method alone.

Soil compaction: Comparatively low under mob stocking in 2015. Rotational grazing showed
highest compaction, but this reflected pre-existing site conditions rather than the grazing
treatment itself. None of the systems showed compaction severe enough to reduce forage
growth.

Nutrient distribution near water sources: This is a key finding. Under continuous and
rotational grazing, nitrogen mineralization (a measure of plant-available N) was nearly
TWICE as high near water sources (0-10m) compared to mob grazing, where N mineralization
was relatively constant across the pasture. This suggests mob grazing's high stocking
density reduces cattle congregation near water/shade areas, spreading manure and urine
nutrients more evenly across the pasture rather than concentrating them near water.

Animal performance (measured only at SVAREC): Cows in mob-grazed pastures were
significantly lighter at breeding, especially in 2015, with lower body condition scores.
Calf weaning weights were consistently lower in mob-grazed pastures (though birth weights
were actually lowest in rotational systems). Hypothesis: long rest periods in mob paddocks
meant tall fescue plants (70-90% of grasses) had gone to seed before grazing, and tall
fescue seed carries the highest alkaloid toxin concentration in the plant - possibly reducing
milk production and cow condition. Reduced available forage from trampling may have also
contributed.

SUMMARY & CONCLUSIONS

- Mob-grazed pastures accumulate more standing forage, but a significant portion is
  trampled and never eaten.
- Forage quality in mob-grazed pastures was reasonably good despite over-maturity,
  probably suitable for dry (non-lactating) cows.
- Contrary to expectations, mob grazing did not suppress clover establishment -
  especially red clover.
- Little difference found in soil health indices across grazing systems over the
  3-year study period, though mob grazing showed more even nutrient distribution
  away from water sources.
- Cow-calf performance was significantly POORER under mob grazing in 2014-2015,
  likely linked to toxic tall fescue seed consumption and reduced available forage.

BOTTOM LINE: This Virginia Tech study found little evidence to support broad, year-round
adoption of mob grazing over standard rotational grazing in tall-fescue-based systems.
Mob grazing may be better suited to specific, short-term management tasks (e.g.
vegetation/weed control) rather than as a full-time strategy - particularly in regions
where tall fescue dominates the sward, due to the toxicosis risk from mature seed heads.
