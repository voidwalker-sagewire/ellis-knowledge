WHY TEMPORARY WATER/FENCE MATTERS

Research shows pasture is poorly utilized beyond 800-1,000 feet from a watering source,
and perhaps even less on steep terrain. Temporary water troughs and electric fencing let
farms intensify management as needed: subdividing pastures with frequent moves, using
large pastures more effectively, or bringing new acreage into the grazing rotation.

TEMPORARY WATERING OPTIONS

Most common approach: tap into an existing pressurized water source (hydrant or
permanent waterer) and run 3/4" or 1" plastic polyethylene (PE) pipe on top of the
ground to the desired area. Pipe is often run beneath a division fence to protect it
from animal traffic. Valves or quick couplers (e.g., the Plasson Quick Coupling Valve)
installed at regular intervals along the pipe let a portable trough be relocated
throughout the field.

Examples of setups seen in the field:
- A permanent waterer mounted on runners, pulled with a four-wheeler
- A gravity-fed stock tank with a floor-mounted float valve
- Tapping a pressurized waterer with aboveground PE pipe to extend service to an
  adjacent field
- Commercial "drag trough" systems (e.g., Kiwitech 100-liter Drag Trough, Kiwitech
  Micro Trough) designed for portability

SOLAR-POWERED PUMPING (for surface water sources)

Where no pressurized water source is available, solar power can enable use of surface
water (ponds, creeks) for more intensive grazing management. Two common setups:
1. Solar panel powers a submersible or surface-mounted pump that fills a reservoir,
   which then gravity-feeds a stock tank fitted with a float valve.
2. Solar panel charges a battery bank, which powers the pump for direct filling of a
   stock trough.

Some farms mount the reservoir on a wagon and the stock tank on runners for full
portability - e.g., a solar pump placed in a nearby creek fills a wagon-mounted
reservoir that gravity-flows to a movable stock tank with a float valve.

Cost guidance: solar panels and compatible DC pumps have become widely available and
affordable. Farms with LOW water demand (under 2,000 gallons/day) and LOW lift
requirements (15 feet or less) can generally get a pump + solar panel for a reasonable
cost. Troughs, reservoirs, and plumbing add to total cost and vary by situation. Water
demand over 2,000 gal/day and/or lift over 15 feet significantly increases pump and
solar array costs. "Plug-and-play" solar watering kits are commercially available.

FREEZE PROTECTION FOR ABOVEGROUND WATER

Aboveground watering components are generally limited to spring-through-fall use.
Safeguards can extend usable season by a couple of weeks on either end:
- High-density polyethylene (HDPE) or cross-linked polyethylene (PEX) pipe/valves
  (more freeze-tolerant than standard PE)
- Specialized temperature-activated drip valves (e.g., the commercially available
  "Freeze Miser," or similar thermal wax actuators) that open automatically to keep
  water moving and prevent freezing

TEMPORARY ELECTRIC FENCING

Products: step-in posts, poly wire or poly tape (plastic twine/ribbon with conductive
wire filaments), and electric netting - all designed for quick install/removal.

Temporary electric fencing is NOT a physical barrier - it's a PSYCHOLOGICAL barrier that
works well for as-needed subdivision of existing permanent paddocks under high-intensity
management. Common uses:
- Strip-grazing stockpiled pasture, rotating livestock every 1-3 days to increase
  forage utilization
- Excluding livestock from sensitive areas (a seasonal bog, a newly seeded area)

Due to its flexibility, temporary electric fencing has become the tool of choice for
many high-intensity grazing operations.

PRACTICAL NOTES FROM FARMERS

Using temporary fencing requires at least moderate physical fitness and time investment.
However, producers report:
- More docile livestock when moved frequently under an intensive rotation
- Better ability to track changing pasture conditions in real time
- Time spent moving temporary fences is more than offset by reduced time spent making,
  moving, and feeding hay when the grazing season is extended this way

COST-SHARE AND TECHNICAL SUPPORT (Virginia-specific, but illustrates the general model)

Soil and Water Conservation Districts (SWCDs) and NRCS offices commonly offer technical
support and cost-sharing for grazing infrastructure: interior fencing, waterers, stream
exclusion fencing, and other agronomic practices. Worth checking whether an equivalent
program exists locally/regionally, as these programs substantially reduce upfront
infrastructure costs for producers transitioning to intensive rotational grazing.
