WHAT IS REGENERATIVE AGRICULTURE?

A system of farming principles that rehabilitates the entire ecosystem and enhances
natural resources rather than depleting them. Robert Rodale coined "regenerative" to
distinguish it from merely "sustainable" farming - regenerative agriculture "takes advantage
of the natural tendencies of ecosystems to regenerate when disturbed."

Marked by: closed nutrient loops, reduced/eliminated biocidal chemicals, greater crop and
biological diversity, fewer annuals and more perennials, and practices that mimic natural
ecological processes.

CORE REGENERATIVE PRACTICES SUPPORTING SOIL CARBON SEQUESTRATION

- Diversifying crop rotations
- Planting cover crops, green manures, and perennials
- Retaining crop residues
- Using natural sources of fertilizer (compost)
- Employing highly managed grazing and/or integrating crops and livestock
- Reducing tillage frequency and depth
- Eliminating synthetic chemicals

These practices alone do not signify regeneration - they are a starting point. The
synergy between interlinked practices, not any single one, is what drives soil biodiversity
and carbon sequestration.

BIODIVERSITY BELOW GROUND (SOIL LIFE)

Soil life is exceptionally complex: billions of organisms in just one teaspoon of healthy
soil, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, plus larger fauna like arthropods,
earthworms, and springtails.

A systematic review of 50+ international studies found nearly 60% more biomass from
soil microorganisms in organically managed systems vs. conventional, and soil life in
organic systems was over 80% more active.

KEY INSIGHT: It's not recalcitrant plant material that persists to build long-term soil
carbon - it's the MICROBES that process plant matter that are most responsible for carbon
sequestration. Stable soil carbon is formed mostly by microbial necromass (dead microbial
biomass) bonded to minerals (silt and clay) in the soil. This protection happens in soil
pores of 30-150 micrometers, created by roots from diverse polycultures - not monocrops.

Practical implication: to build soil carbon, farmers should focus on encouraging diverse
carbon inputs (to create pore structures) and feeding soil microbes - both achieved
through a wide variety of living plant roots. Roots feed microbes directly via exudates
(sugars, amino acids, organic acids) and partner with mycorrhizal fungi.

Quote from soil ecologist Francesca Cotrufo (Colorado State University): "It's becoming
very clear that in order to regenerate soils, we have to have continuous and diverse
inputs, and that mostly comes from living roots."

CARBON-TO-NITROGEN RATIO MATTERS

Soil has a conservative carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of about 10:1 - every 10 units of carbon
require 1 unit of nitrogen for sequestration to occur. This is why high-carbon but
low-quality inputs (e.g. high proportion of sawdust/woodchips, or cereal-only cover crops)
result in LOW carbon use efficiency and can put microbes under nitrogen stress, causing
them to mine nitrogen from existing soil organic matter. Better inputs: legume cover
crops, manure, vegetable-based or worm compost (low C:N ratio, high quality).

NITROGEN SOURCE MATTERS FOR CARBON STORAGE

Compost/manure fertility results in increased soil carbon storage; synthetic fertilizer
results in loss or no change in soil carbon. When compost replaces synthetic nitrogen,
plants grow more roots, fixing more atmospheric carbon. Legume cover crops were found
to be twice as efficient at storing soil organic carbon as nitrogen fertilization. After
34 years in Rodale's Farming Systems Trial, the organic manure system had 18-21% higher
soil organic carbon than the conventional system.

FUNGI

90% of all plants live in symbiosis with mycorrhizal fungi. These fungi receive plant
belowground carbon as their energy source and in return provide up to 80% of a plant's
nitrogen and phosphorus. Mycorrhizal fungi secrete glomalin, a protein responsible for
creating persistent, stable soil aggregates that protect carbon from being released as
CO2. Farming strategies that support mycorrhizal fungi: perennial plantings, trees on
edges, reduced tillage, and plants with long fibrous root systems.

BIODIVERSITY ABOVE GROUND

Bare soil disables photosynthesis and encourages erosion. Monocultures require chemical
inputs that destroy soil biology. Only nine crops account for ~70% of worldwide
agricultural land use (sugar cane, maize, rice, wheat, potatoes, soybeans, oil-palm,
sugar beet, cassava) - mostly grown in narrow rotations, making farms vulnerable to
pest outbreaks and extreme weather.

Moving from monoculture-with-fallow to polyculture-with-no-fallow increases soil
biodiversity and sequesters carbon. Switching wheat-fallow to wheat-sunflower or
wheat-legume rotation significantly increased soil organic carbon; continuous barley
more than doubled soil carbon vs. barley-fallow.

Important nuance on tillage: a 30-year Brazil trial found legume cover crop effect on
soil carbon was GREATER than the effect of no-till alone. In Rodale's own Farming Systems
Trial, soil carbon differences were not driven by tillage intensity but by organic vs.
conventional management. After 10 years of continuous no-till, the CONVENTIONAL no-till
system had the LOWEST soil organic carbon of all six systems tested - meaning no-till
alone, without cover crops and diverse rotations, does not sequester carbon. It slows
carbon loss but doesn't build it.

MULCHES AND COMPOST

Retaining crop residues as mulch: prevents erosion, inhibits weeds, moderates soil
temperature, reduces water evaporation, feeds earthworms, protects from extreme weather.

Compost increases soil biodiversity and microbial biomass, improving structure, nutrient
cycling, and disease suppression. Benefits can accrue quickly - after just one compost
application, soil organic carbon and aggregate stability can increase significantly in
following years. In a 10-year trial, fields amended with composted dairy manure
sequestered over 2 metric tons of carbon/hectare/year while the paired conventional
system LOST carbon.

Caveat: relying on transported/composted manure for cropland carbon sequestration can be
limited by supply and transport costs. On-farm and local waste-stream composting that
recycles nutrients naturally is preferred where feasible.

REDUCING TILLAGE

Plowing breaks up aggregates, destroys fungal networks, increases water evaporation,
accelerates organic matter breakdown, and can cause erosion. But research shows
conventional no-till ALONE (without cover crops/rotation) often does not sequester
carbon - it must be part of a systems approach. Organic systems using REDUCED tillage
(not necessarily zero-till) combined with heavy cover cropping for weed suppression have
shown soil organic carbon increases of 9% after two years, 20%+ after six years.

GRAZING MANAGEMENT

Grazing lands account for more than 70% of global agricultural land area (1.4 billion
hectares of cropland vs. 3.3 billion hectares of pasture/meadow) - meaning grazing land
management may offer the single greatest carbon sequestration opportunity in agriculture.

"Regenerative grazing" is an umbrella term covering adaptive multi-paddock (AMP) grazing,
holistic grazing management, and mob grazing. Their commonality: frequent, calculated
movement of high densities of ruminants, with decisions based on herd size and forage
quality/quantity available. This mimics large migratory herds in nature and allows forage
to recover between grazing events - which can increase soil organic carbon even at
stocking densities once thought detrimental to soil health under continuous/set-stocking
systems.

In integrated crop-livestock systems, a nine-year Brazilian study found greater soil
organic carbon and nitrogen stocks under MODERATE and LIGHT grazing intensity
(20-40 cm forage height at removal) than under ungrazed or high-intensity grazing -
i.e., there's a sweet spot; overgrazing undermines the carbon benefit.

More diverse pasture grass mixes, especially those including legumes, sequester carbon
better than less diverse pastures, and some research links this diversity to REDUCED
methane emissions from cattle.

A Southern US study found dairy farms converting from degraded conventional row-crop to
regenerative grazing began sequestering carbon at 4.6 metric tons/hectare/year within
2 years, rising to 9 metric tons/year before plateauing and declining after 6 years -
demonstrating that stable soil carbon CAN be built quickly upon transition to regenerative
grazing.

CARBON DEPTH MATTERS

Carbon is more likely to be protected deeper in the subsoil (1-2 meters), yet most
soil carbon sampling only measures the top 30-40cm - meaning current datasets likely
UNDERESTIMATE soil carbon stocks, especially in systems that redistribute carbon deeper
via root activity rather than surface accumulation.

THE YIELD QUESTION

Meta-analyses show organic yields average lower than conventional overall - but this gap
is most pronounced when organic practices simply substitute organic-approved inputs for
conventional chemical inputs, rather than taking a true systems/holistic approach. In
well-designed regenerative organic systems, yields have outcompeted conventional for
corn, wheat, rice, soybean, and sunflower in documented trials. In 2016, Rodale's organic
no-till-with-manure system produced 200 bushels of corn/acre - a record for the system,
above both the county average and that year's conventional yield (140 bu/acre). Over
Rodale's 40-year trial, no statistically significant yield difference existed between
organic and conventional overall.

Critically, organic/regenerative systems show much greater RESILIENCE during drought
years - 30% to 100% higher yields than conventional during drought in the Rodale trial.
This climate resilience is an underappreciated economic co-benefit.

KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR A PRACTICAL OPERATION

1. Living roots in the ground as much of the year as possible is the central organizing
   principle - this feeds soil microbes and builds the pore structure that protects carbon.
2. Diversity above ground (crop/forage species diversity) drives diversity and carbon
   storage below ground.
3. Compost and organic nitrogen sources outperform synthetic fertilizer for building
   soil carbon.
4. No-till alone, without diverse rotations/cover cropping, does not reliably sequester
   carbon - it's the combination of practices that matters.
5. Well-managed rotational/mob-style grazing with adequate rest periods can build soil
   carbon rapidly, but overgrazing (or under-resting) undermines the benefit - moderate
   grazing intensity outperformed both no grazing and heavy grazing in at least one
   integrated system study.
6. Regenerative systems show strong resilience advantages during drought - a major
   practical benefit beyond carbon accounting alone.
