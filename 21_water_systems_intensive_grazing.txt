WHY A GOOD WATER SYSTEM MATTERS

"Development of a good pasture watering system can make or break a grazing system."
Key considerations: water quality and quantity, supply equipment, groundwater
protection, and human/animal safety. Rotational/management-intensive grazing systems
are NOT effective if water isn't readily available to every paddock - this is
consistently the infrastructure bottleneck that limits how intensively a system can
actually be managed.

WATER QUANTITY REQUIREMENTS

Beef cattle water needs vary dramatically by season:
- Winter: roughly 9 gallons per 1,000 lbs bodyweight per day
- Hot summer days: nearly 30 gallons per 1,000 lbs bodyweight per day
(over a 3x swing between winter and peak summer demand - system sizing needs to
account for the hot-weather case, not an average)

General reference table (gallons/head/day, varies by source and animal class):
lactating dairy cows need meaningfully MORE water than dry cows or growing stock -
one Cornell study found lactating Holsteins need roughly 5 additional gallons/day
beyond baseline maintenance needs. Always size for the highest-demand class actually
present in the herd.

DISTANCE TO WATER - THE KEY DESIGN PARAMETER

Ideally, distance to water should be no more than about 800 feet from any point in a
paddock (other sources cite a 300-500 ft or 600-900 ft range depending on grazing
intensity - shorter distances are better and become more important as grazing
intensity increases). Pasture is poorly utilized beyond this range regardless of
forage quality, since cattle won't walk far enough for water and back to fully use
distant forage.

Placing the tank within 500-800 feet of the paddock means cattle visit the tank ONE AT
A TIME or in small numbers, rather than the whole herd converging at once - this
significantly reduces the demand/flow-rate burden on the system compared to
continuous grazing where the whole herd may drink together.

TANK SIZING AND EQUIPMENT

Small tanks (20-50 gallons) are common and workable for intensive grazing, AS LONG AS
valve sizing and pipe sizing keep the tank level near full while cattle drink - this
also reduces the risk of cattle tipping smaller tanks over. Only a slow/intermittent
supply source (e.g. a direct-connect solar pump with no battery buffer) requires a
much larger tank holding a full day's water supply or more, to bridge gaps in supply.

Tank valve selection: inexpensive float valves may only supply 2-3 gallons/minute -
often insufficient once 2+ animals are drinking simultaneously at a small tank.
Full-flow floats can deliver up to 20 gpm with proper pipe/system design - worth the
modest extra cost for busier tanks. A bottom-inlet float device controls water level
while staying out of cattle's reach (reduces damage/fouling risk).

Energy-free or electrically-heated permanent waterers: size for one watering space
(roughly 1 cup or 2 lineal feet of tank) per 25 head.

WELLHEAD AND GROUNDWATER PROTECTION

Protect wells/groundwater with proper wellhead construction (check state health
department guidelines); seal abandoned wells properly. Any tank/waterer fed by well
water or a water district pipeline should have a vacuum-break or backflow prevention
device - this prevents tank water (potentially contaminated by livestock) from
siphoning back INTO the water supply line if line pressure drops. Most top-mounted
commercial float valves include an air gap or anti-siphon feature, but local plumbing
codes/health regulations may require specific additional backflow prevention
equipment.

WATER SOURCE OPTIONS (in rough order of complexity/cost)

PONDS AND STREAMS: Can supply water, but it's desirable to FENCE cattle away from
direct surface-water access where possible, allowing only a small controlled access
point (or none at all) - this protects water quality, prevents bank erosion, and
improves riparian habitat. A good alternative: pump or gravity-feed water FROM the
pond/stream to a separate pasture tank, keeping cattle off the water body entirely.

SIPHONS: A gravity-feed system usable when a pond/static source isn't far from the
target pasture and there's sufficient elevation drop. The pipe must encounter a rise
between inlet and outlet (going up and over the pond bank), with the outlet ending
LOWER than the pond's water level. The pipe must be guaranteed leak-proof from the
pond waterline up, or the siphon loses its prime. Friction losses in the pipe must be
accounted for in delivery calculations. A float valve at the tank controls the
system; a floating inlet or gravel screen inlet at the pond end is used, though any
screen adds some pressure drop.

RAM PUMPS: Usable in rare cases with a spring-fed stream that has adequate flow and
gravity head - delivers water UPHILL from the stream with NO external power source
required (a mechanical pump powered entirely by the flowing water's own energy). Only
delivers a fraction of the total water flowing through it - one manufacturer example
cited: a 1-foot drop to a 10-foot lift delivers roughly 15-20% of the water used by
the pump. Check manufacturer specs before relying on this for a full herd's water
needs.

SOLAR-POWERED PUMPS: Useful where a water source exists but no electric utility power
is nearby. Systems are typically set up with a LARGE tank (up to 5 days' supply) to
buffer through cloudy periods when solar output drops. Economics improve or worsen
based on well depth/lift required and flow rate needed - deeper wells or higher lifts
make solar pumping more expensive, so it's not favorable in every situation. Contact
local extension or NRCS for system sizing help; the technology itself is
well-established with multiple equipment sources available.

WIND-POWERED PUMPS: Generally less reliable than solar during the core May-September
grazing season in many regions (wind resource is more variable seasonally than
solar). A hybrid wind/solar system may pencil out for an extended grazing season in
some situations.

NOSE PUMPS (cattle-operated, mechanical): Good for lifting water up to about 20 feet
over fairly short distances. The animal itself pumps water a stroke at a time via a
piston/valve mechanism activated by pushing the plunger while drinking. Only one
animal can access at a time, limiting practicality for larger herds - figure roughly
25 head served per nose pump.

SHALLOW-WELL SUCTION PUMPS: The simplest well pump type. Limited by maximum suction
lift (depth to water plus friction head in the suction line) - theoretically
atmospheric pressure allows nearly 30 feet of lift, but practically the reliable limit
is more like 15-20 feet. A good foot valve is essential to avoid losing prime when the
pump shuts off; many shallow-well pumps are not self-priming. Whether a shallow-well
or deep-well pump is needed depends on the well's actual draw-down under real
cattle-watering demand conditions, not just static water table depth.

ELECTRIC POWER SUPPLY FOR PUMPS

Requires adequately-sized wiring to maintain sufficient voltage at the pump motor -
undersized wire or a long wire run causes voltage drop, which can overheat and
prematurely fail the motor. Wire sizing depends on full-load motor amps (FLA) and
wire run length; consult an electrical reference (e.g. Midwest Plan Service MWPS-28,
Farm Buildings Wiring Handbook) for sizing tables and proper grounding practices for
pumps and heated waterers.

PIPE SELECTION

For GRAVITY-FLOW systems: linear low-density polyethylene (LLDPE) pipe is sufficient.
For PRESSURIZED systems: use rolled high-density polyethylene (HDPE) rated for a
minimum 150 PSI. Use a pressure-flow chart to select the correct minimum pipe size for
the expected flow rate - undersized pipe creates excessive friction loss and
inadequate delivery at the tank (see friction-loss reference table below).

SETTING UP THE SYSTEM - PRACTICAL SEQUENCING ADVICE

Start with a PORTABLE, FLEXIBLE layout. Lay pipe on top of the ground initially, while
still refining paddock layout and water placement - only bury pipe once the system
configuration is confirmed as final. A common approach: install a main trunk line
underground once confirmed, with risers and quick-disconnects feeding movable
tanks at various points.

Economic payback note: analyses of grazing systems consistently show that money spent
providing water to multiple locations (or every paddock) pays back rapidly through
improved forage utilization and grazing distribution - this is a genuinely
high-ROI infrastructure investment, not a luxury.

OTHER PRACTICAL NOTES

- Black pipe on top of the ground will heat water somewhat in direct sun. This is
  usually NOT a problem in summer since cattle actually prefer/best-use water near
  rumen temperature anyway. Running pipe under taller forage/vegetation, or under a
  fence line, provides natural shading that minimizes solar heating if desired.
- Temporary/mobile tanks can be placed under an electric fence line to keep cattle
  pressure off the equipment and reduce tank tip-overs.
- Moving portable tank location each time a paddock is regrazed helps prevent
  concentrated forage kill and mud problems from repeatedly using the exact same spot.
- Permanent tank areas should be graveled or otherwise treated for all-weather access
  - a combination of geotextile fabric covered with gravel creates a stable base and
  prevents the area from becoming a mud pit.

REFERENCE TABLES

Friction loss in feet per 100 feet of plastic pipe (selected values, by pipe nominal
size and flow rate):
- 4 gpm: 3/4" pipe = 3.7 ft loss; 1" = 1.2 ft; 1.25" = 0.3 ft; 1.5" = 0.1 ft
- 8 gpm: 1" pipe = 4.1 ft loss; 1.25" = 1.1 ft; 1.5" = 0.5 ft
- 12 gpm: 1.25" pipe = 2.3 ft loss; 1.5" = 1.1 ft; 2" = 0.3 ft
- 20 gpm: 1.5" pipe = 2.6 ft loss; 2" = 0.8 ft
(Higher flow rates need progressively larger pipe to keep friction loss manageable -
undersizing pipe for the target flow rate is a common practical mistake.)

Feet-of-head to PSI conversion (useful for gravity-flow/elevation-drop calculations):
- 10 ft of head = 4.33 PSI
- 20 ft of head = 8.66 PSI
- 30 ft of head = 12.99 PSI
- 50 ft of head = 21.66 PSI
Reverse (PSI to feet of head):
- 10 PSI = 23.10 ft
- 20 PSI = 46.20 ft
- 30 PSI = 69.30 ft
- 50 PSI = 115.5 ft

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY FOR ELLIS

The core design question for any water system upgrade is: what's the elevation
relationship and distance between the water source and where cattle need to drink?
That single relationship determines whether gravity/siphon (cheapest, no power
needed), a nose pump (cheap, low-volume, single-animal), a ram pump (free power but
narrow use case), or a powered pump (solar/electric, most flexible but highest cost)
is the right tool. Distance-to-water under ~800 feet from any paddock point is the
target that actually drives full forage utilization - everything else is in service
of hitting that number affordably.
