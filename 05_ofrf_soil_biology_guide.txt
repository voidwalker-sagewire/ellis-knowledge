THE SOIL FOOD WEB - OVERVIEW

The soil biota (soil life) has total live weight of 1,700-27,000 lb/acre yet accounts for just
1-5% of total soil organic matter (SOM) - and regulates essentially the entire stock of SOM.
Nearly all stable SOM ("humus") is now understood to be derived from microbial processing
of plant residues - largely microbial remains and metabolites tightly bonded to soil minerals.

KEY PRINCIPLE: "If you feed the soil, the soil will feed the plant." Many farmers consider
soil life their "underground livestock" - it must be fed, watered, and sheltered just like
above-ground herds.

SOIL FUNCTIONS AND KEY ORGANISMS (summary table)

- Plant nutrition (retaining/recycling nutrients): decomposer bacteria/fungi, earthworms,
  arthropods
- Plant nutrition (delivering nutrients to plants): protozoa, nematodes, N-fixing bacteria,
  mycorrhizal fungi
- Moisture/drainage/aeration: bacteria (glues), fungi (mycelia), earthworms, arthropods,
  plant roots
- Crop protection (pathogen/pest deterrence): pathogen antagonists, predators/parasites
  of pests
- Water quality: plant roots and cover, nutrient-immobilizing microbes
- Carbon sequestration: plant roots, fungi, bacteria, deep-burrowing earthworms
- Erosion resistance: plant cover/roots, fungal hyphae, bacterial glues
- Resilience/tilth recovery after disturbance: plant roots, earthworms, arthropods, fungi

ORGANIC PRACTICES TO BUILD SOIL LIFE

- Adding compost and other organic materials
- Diversifying crop rotation
- Growing cover crops
- Utilizing legumes for nitrogen
- Integrating crops and livestock
- Limiting tillage, pesticide sprays, and concentrated fertilizer inputs

A meta-analysis of 56 worldwide studies found organically managed soils maintained
32-84% greater microbial biomass and enzyme activity than the same soils managed
conventionally.

USDA NRCS FOUR PRINCIPLES OF SOIL HEALTH (cited as foundational roadmap):
1. Keep soil covered
2. Maintain living roots
3. Diversify crops
4. Minimize soil disturbance
(A commonly cited 5th principle, per rancher/author Gabe Brown: integrate livestock)

THE ROLE OF PLANT ROOTS

No soil ecosystem survives without living plants - the "solar collector" all terrestrial
life depends on. Roots occupy roughly 1% of soil volume but account for a quarter to a
third of total soil respiration, and create the "rhizosphere" - an enriched habitat for
soil organisms. Prolonged fallow periods without living roots put soil life on a
"starvation diet," depleting SOM and harming soil health.

In healthy soils with low-to-moderate soluble nutrients, plants may contribute 20-40% of
their photosynthetic product to soil life through root exudates, mycorrhizae, and other
symbiotic relationships. Legumes may invest ~20% of production feeding rhizobia bacteria.
IMPORTANT: when crops are heavily fertilized with readily available N/P, they share LESS
energy with soil microbiota - saving short-term plant energy but limiting soil biological
activity and potentially harming long-term soil health.

Annual crops send ~25% of photosynthetic product below ground into roots 3-6 feet deep.
Perennial prairie plants and forest trees send roughly HALF their photosynthetic product
into roots that may extend 15+ feet - roots are now believed to be the primary source of
stable SOM (more than surface residue).

MYCORRHIZAL FUNGI

Associate with about 80% of plant species, including 70% of agricultural crops. Fossil
evidence shows they co-evolved with the earliest land plants 400+ million years ago.
Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) form structures called arbuscules inside root tissue
where nutrient exchange occurs. In a grass-legume mix, a single AMF network can literally
"ship" legume-fixed nitrogen to the grass and surplus phosphorus from the grass back to
the legume - a four-way symbiosis (grass + legume + rhizobia + AMF) that enhances forage
vigor in pasture.

TILLAGE'S EFFECT ON SOIL LIFE

Tillage strongly affects soil life: plowing "turns homes upside down," disking/rotary
tillage pulverizes aggregates and destroys microbial habitat, kills earthworms, and
fragments fungal mycelia. It stimulates bacterial growth and speeds mineralization
(short-term nutrient release) at the cost of long-term stabilization.

Tools that mitigate tillage impact: chisel plow or shallow (3") tillage retain more
microbial biomass than moldboard plow or disking. Ridge/strip tillage stimulates
mineralization in the crop row while leaving SOM and fungi intact between rows
("soil functional zone management").

GRAZING AND LIVESTOCK INTEGRATION

Direct quote: "Integrating crops and livestock, an important principle since the
beginning of the organic movement, can enhance soil life if grazing is managed well.
While continuous grazing degrades pastures, management intensive rotational grazing
(MIG), in which brief (1-3 days) intense grazing is followed by a sufficient recovery
period (1-6 months depending on climate, season, and pasture condition), enhances forage
vigor, root growth, rhizodeposition, and dramatically increases SOM."

TABLE: EFFECTS OF FARMING PRACTICES ON SOIL ORGANISMS AND FUNCTIONS (key excerpts)

- Manure: increases bacteria, protozoa, bacteria-feeding nematodes, SOM (if moderate C:N);
  can decrease mycorrhizal fungi if phosphorus builds up
- Compost: increases SOM stabilization; can decrease mycorrhizal fungi if P builds up
- Diverse rotation without fallow: increases SOM, microbial diversity, nutrient cycling
- Cover crops: increase microbial biomass, active SOM, soil aggregation, mycorrhizal fungi
  (with grasses/legumes); can increase N leaching risk
- Sod phase in rotation: increases active/stable SOM, biomass and diversity of most soil
  organisms, tilth, nutrient cycling; decreases N leaching, erosion, compaction
- Unplanted fallow: increases soil erosion, compaction, nutrient loss; decreases ALL soil
  life, especially plant symbionts, SOM, nutrient cycling
- Livestock integration: increases active/total SOM, nutrient cycling, most soil organisms;
  can decrease mycorrhizal fungi if phosphorus excesses accumulate
- Routine tillage (1+ pass/year): increases bacteria, protozoa, mineralization; decreases
  most fungi, earthworms, other macrofauna, SOM stabilization
- Intensive tillage: increases erosion and metabolic stress in microbes; decreases ALL
  soil life, SOM, tilth
- No-till/reduced till: increases fungi, macrofauna, SOM stabilization; can decrease
  mineralization of SOM/N and some bacteria

CROP-PREFERRED MICROBIOMES

Different crops prefer different soil microbiomes:
- Crucifers (cabbage family) and chenopods (beets, spinach, quinoa family): prefer
  bacteria-dominated soil, do NOT benefit from mycorrhizal fungi. Favored by moderate
  tillage, near-neutral pH, succulent green manures.
- Grains, legumes, forages, most vegetables (allium, solanaceous): thrive with a
  BALANCED mix of bacteria and fungi including AMF. Favored by reduced tillage, mixed
  cover crops, moderate C:N inputs (~20:1).
- Fruit/nut trees, grapevines, other woody perennials: prefer FUNGAL-dominated soil,
  often depend on ectomycorrhizal fungi specifically. Favored by woody organic
  amendments, undisturbed soil (no tillage), continuous living/mulch ground cover.

BEST MANAGEMENT PRACTICES FOR SOIL LIFE (Concept #2 summary)

- Feed soil biota via plant roots - maximize year-round living plant cover; maintain
  dormant vegetation/residue cover during dry or frozen seasons
- Supplement with finished compost or balanced-C:N amendments
- Diversify: crop rotation, cover/sod crops, intercropping, relay planting, perennial
  plantings
- Integrate livestock with crops using management-intensive rotational grazing (MIG)
- Reduce tillage when practical
- Minimize other disturbances: excessive N/P, fallow without cover, invasive exotic
  plants
- Adjust practices for site-specific factors (climate, rainfall, soil type, production
  system)

BIOLOGICAL DISEASE MANAGEMENT

Healthy, biologically active soils with ample SOM generally reduce plant disease
problems - abundant benign microbes crowd out pathogens ("general disease
suppressiveness"), and biodiverse soils include more natural enemies of pathogens.
Soil biodiversity can also protect livestock/human health: increased dung beetle and
manure-decomposing bacteria activity on organic farms helps control pathogenic E. coli
and livestock parasites.

Strategies: crop rotation (removes susceptible host long enough to control some
pathogens), cover crops (roll-crimped mulch protects against soilborne pathogens; deep
roots like sudangrass penetrate hardpan and reduce root rot in following crops),
organic amendments (raw manure or succulent green manures stimulate competing microbial
growth that suppresses pathogens).

MICROBIAL INOCULANTS - A REALITY CHECK

Important finding: A multi-year Ohio State study across 2 research stations and 19
organic farms in 7 states found NO significant yield benefit from 13 commercial
biostimulant/inoculant products tested on 7 vegetable crops. Reasons inoculants often
fail: indigenous soil organisms already perform the function; indigenous organisms
outcompete the added microbes; soil conditions don't favor the applied organisms;
storage/application problems; or the crop-microbe partnership requirement isn't met.

Rancher Gabe Brown restored 5,000 degraded acres near Bismarck, ND (16"/year rainfall,
starting at 2% organic matter) using cover crops, diversified rotation, no-till, and
rotational grazing - building SOM to 7% over 20 years WITHOUT applying a single
microbial inoculant. His quote: "No matter what you do to the soil, there will still be
some small bit of life in it, even in the most chemically dependent or heavily tilled
operations. If you give that life a chance to grow, it will respond... If you build it...
or if you stop destroying it, they will come."

Takeaway: inoculants are most likely to help on moderately low-fertility soils where the
applied microbes can actually thrive; on healthy/fertile soils, they often add cost
without benefit. Legume rhizobia inoculation remains a validated standard practice
when planting a legume in a field without recent history of that legume.

TAXONOMIC TOUR OF SOIL LIFE (condensed)

Bacteria: small (0.5-5 micrometers), 300-4,000 lb/ac live weight. Billions per teaspoon
of topsoil, thousands of genotypes. Fix atmospheric nitrogen (Rhizobium in legume
nodules, free-living Azotobacter, etc.), cycle nutrients, secrete glue-like
polysaccharides that build soil aggregation.

Archaea: similar to bacteria in size/function but genetically distinct domain. Can be
~10% of microbial biomass in upland soils. Oxidize sulfur, break down hydrocarbons.

Actinobacteria (Actinomycetes): filamentous, 350-4,000 lb/ac. Digest cellulose and chitin
that resist other bacteria. Responsible for the "earthy" smell of healthy soil. Thrive
in warm, moist, near-neutral soil (pH 6.0-7.5) but tolerate drought/salinity well.

Fungi: 900-10,000 lb/ac, often half or more of soil microbial biomass. Convert consumed
organic material into new biomass far more efficiently than bacteria (up to 50% vs ~20%),
making them a leading source of stable SOM. Two functional groups: saprophytic
(decomposer) fungi and mycorrhizal fungi.

Protozoa: 18-250 lb/ac. Prey mostly on bacteria; release surplus nitrogen as
plant-available ammonium. Key role in delivering plant-available nitrogen from the
bacteria-rich rhizosphere.

Nematodes: 10-260 lb/ac. Tiny worms occupying diverse niches. Nutrient release from
nematodes feeding on bacteria/fungi may account for 30-40% of plant-available nitrogen
in some soils. Only a minority are crop pests (root-feeders); predatory and
entomopathogenic nematodes provide pest control services.

Micro-arthropods (mites, springtails): 4-450 lb/ac. Shred plant litter, speeding
microbial access and decomposition. Highly sensitive to tillage - far more abundant in
sod/pasture than annually-tilled cropland.

Earthworms: 90-3,500 lb/ac live weight, can process twice their own weight in soil daily.
In a climate supporting 6 months of earthworm activity, worms can turn over 9-450 tons
of soil per acre annually - effectively performing "biological tillage." Large earthworm
populations contribute 45-80 lb of plant-available nitrogen per acre per year just from
their own die-off and decay.

Dung beetles: In grazing systems, consume and bury manure in topsoil, conserving and
cycling nutrients, protecting surface water quality, and reducing pathogen/parasite
loads. Grazing ecosystems that have lost native dung beetle populations show markedly
improved vegetative growth after appropriate species are reintroduced. Directly relevant
to rotational/mob grazing management - a healthy dung beetle population is a strong
positive indicator.

PRACTICAL MONITORING METHODS FOR FARMERS (from simplest to more advanced)

- Visual assessment: soil color, tilth/aggregation, surface porosity (rain infiltration),
  drainage, resistance to root growth
- Crop performance and resilience to disease/drought/stress
- Tracking total SOM trends on soil test reports over time
- Crop nutrition needs from soil (how much fertilizer required to sustain yield)
- Earthworm counts and visible fungal mycelia
- Lab tests: POX-C (active carbon), PMC (potentially mineralizable carbon via 4-day
  respiration test), or a comprehensive soil health test such as the Cornell CASH
  framework
