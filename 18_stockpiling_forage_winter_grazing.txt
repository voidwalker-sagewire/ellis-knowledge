WHAT IS STOCKPILING (aka "deferred grazing")

The practice of removing livestock from a pasture or hayfield in late summer and
letting forage accumulate through fall, then grazing it later in the fall/winter
instead of feeding hay. It's one of the single highest-leverage cost reductions
available to a cow-calf operation, since winter feed (hay) is typically the most
expensive time of year to feed animals.

WHY TALL FESCUE IS THE STANDOUT SPECIES FOR STOCKPILING

Tall fescue is widely considered the best stockpiling species where it's adapted,
for several converging reasons:
- Grows well late into fall and accumulates substantial yield when conditions favor it
- Remains green and stays high in digestibility/palatability even after frost - its
  leaves don't deteriorate as fast as other stockpiled forages once cold weather hits
- Forms a good sod, making it tolerant of treading/trampling even on saturated winter
  soils - grazing it hard in winter has minimal impact on the following season's
  productivity
- In response to short days and cool nights, tall fescue accumulates HIGH soluble
  carbohydrate levels in both leaves and stems - up to 20% of dry weight as free sugars
  - which is why well-managed stockpiled fescue stays leafy, palatable, and high in
  protein/sugar/digestible energy well into winter (protein levels can stay around 12%
  even into February)
- The heavy waxy leaf cuticle also makes stockpiled fescue notably freeze-resistant
- Cattle will graze stockpiled tall fescue through fresh snow up to 8 inches deep

TIMING - WHEN TO START STOCKPILING

Start window: mid-July through mid-August is commonly cited as ideal (one Kansas
source specifically emphasizes early September through mid-November as their regional
window - timing shifts somewhat by latitude/climate, so check local extension guidance).
General principle: the EARLIER stockpiling starts, the greater the total late-fall/
winter YIELD will be - but earlier-started stockpiles will be somewhat LOWER in
quality than later-started ones, since more of the growth period overlaps warmer
weather. If fescue is stockpiled starting before July, yield ends up about the same
but quality is lower.
Little to no forage growth occurs after mid-October in most regions due to low light
intensity and cooling temperatures - meaning the growth "clock" for a given season's
stockpile effectively runs from the start date to mid-October.

FERTILIZATION FOR STOCKPILING

Maintain soil fertility per a current soil test before stockpiling - this is not
optional if maximizing yield/quality matters. Well-maintained tall fescue responds well
to NITROGEN fertilization early in the stockpiling period IF rainfall is adequate.
Typical nitrogen rate cited: 50-80 lbs of actual N per acre (sources range: one gives
60-80 lbs N/acre, another cites 50 lbs N/acre as sufficient in a successful field
demonstration).
Phosphorus (P2O5) and potassium (K2O) only need to be applied once per year, per soil
test - fall is generally the best timing for that application ahead of a stockpiling
season.
Ammonium nitrate is noted as an excellent nitrogen source specifically because it
minimizes surface volatilization losses compared to urea (urea is cheaper but loses
more N to the atmosphere if not incorporated/rained in promptly).

UTILIZATION - HOW TO ACTUALLY GRAZE IT (this is where most of the value is won or lost)

Critical point: turning livestock loose on a large stockpiled area with unrestricted
access wastes 60-70% of the forage to trampling and manuring on uneaten material.
STRIP GRAZING (restricting daily/multi-day access with temporary poly-wire, moved
progressively across the stockpiled field) is the standard high-utilization method -
restricting access to roughly a 3-day supply of forage at a time can roughly DOUBLE
the number of grazing days obtainable from the same stockpiled acreage compared to
unrestricted access.

WORKED EXAMPLE (grazing days calculation, from Ohio State source)
A 1,200 lb dry beef cow needs ~3% of body weight in dry matter per day = 36 lbs DM/day.
On a field with 4,000 lbs DM/acre stockpiled:
- Poor management (unrestricted access, ~35% utilization): 1,400 lbs available forage
  → 1,400 / 36 = ~39 grazing days per acre
- Managed utilization (~70% utilization via strip grazing): 2,800 lbs available forage
  → 2,800 / 36 = ~78 grazing days per acre
Same field, same total forage grown - nearly DOUBLE the grazing days just from
management method.

A second worked example (Alabama source), using an equivalent per-day intake
calculation for a group: 10 cows x 1,500 lbs x 2% dry matter intake = 300 lbs DM/day
needed for the herd, or 9,000 lbs DM required for a 30-day grazing period. This is the
basic math for sizing how many stockpiled acres a given herd size needs to bridge a
target number of winter days.

GRAZING METHOD COMPARISON (general pattern across sources)

- Continuous stocking: lowest utilization efficiency, most waste
- Moderate rotational stocking: improved utilization over continuous
- Strip grazing (temporary poly-wire moved every 1-3 days): highest utilization,
  most grazing days extracted per acre, and best manure/nutrient distribution across
  the field

FIELD-TESTED PROTOCOL EXAMPLE (Virginia NRCS demonstration, 17.4-acre tall fescue
pasture, goal: 90+ days of stockpile for 60 days or less of winter hay feeding)

1. Graze the designated pasture down to remove summer growth in early-to-mid August
2. Remove livestock and fertilize with ~50 units N/acre after mid-August to boost
   fall growth and quality
3. Let forage grow and accumulate WITHOUT livestock access until the intended grazing
   start date (this demonstration held off until December)
4. Strip-graze the stockpile, moving temporary poly-wire every 3 days
5. Result goals: maximize grazing days, distribute manure/urine nutrients evenly
   across the field, maintain good ground cover, and cut winter feed costs
   substantially (this demonstration reported saving over 50% on winter feed costs)

OTHER STOCKPILING SPECIES BEYOND TALL FESCUE

Bermudagrass is also cited as effective for stockpiling in warmer/transition climates,
typically finished grazing by mid-to-late fall before frost dormancy sets in (unlike
fescue, it doesn't hold quality as well after hard freezes). Both bermudagrass and
tall fescue can meaningfully replace hay requirements in most beef operations when
properly stockpiled and strip-grazed - though supplementation may still be needed for
higher-nutritional-demand animal classes (young/growing stock, late-gestation or
lactating cows) even on good stockpiled forage.

Fescue managed specifically for fall stockpiled growth has been shown to out-yield
sod-seeded annual ryegrass or small grains planted for the same fall/winter window -
worth knowing if choosing between stockpiling an existing perennial stand versus
seeding an annual cover for the same purpose.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS FOR ELLIS TO SURFACE

1. Start planning stockpiling in mid-summer, not fall - by the time cold weather
   arrives it's too late to build meaningful stockpile.
2. A soil test and appropriate nitrogen application are what separate a mediocre
   stockpile from an excellent one - don't skip the fertility step.
3. However the stockpile is grazed, RESTRICTING daily access via strip grazing (not
   turning cattle loose on the whole field) is the single highest-leverage decision -
   it can roughly double usable grazing days from the same forage grown.
4. Test stockpiled forage for quality (protein, digestibility) periodically through
   the winter grazing period so supplementation decisions can be adjusted as forage
   quality naturally declines with age/weathering.
