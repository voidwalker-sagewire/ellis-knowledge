CONTEXT

Cover crops grown for agronomic/soil benefits are increasingly considered for grazing
or haying too - appealing for additional revenue plus soil stabilization benefits. But
some species that work well as cover crops are NOT safe as forage and can be toxic or
fatal to livestock. This is a critical distinction Ellis should always flag: cover crop
suitability and forage/grazing safety are NOT the same evaluation.

METABOLIC DISORDERS (mechanism-based, can occur across many forage types)

BLOAT
Frothy bloat: thick, foamy gas builds in the rumen faster than the animal can belch it
out - can be fatal if pressure isn't relieved.
Prevention: bloat-prevention agents (ionophores, poloxalene) in feed/water; provide dry
roughage before introducing high-risk forage; delay turnout until midday; graze
grass-legume MIXTURES rather than pure legume stands; avoid pastures with more than 50%
bloat-causing legumes.

GLUCOSINOLATES
Natural bitter/"hot"-tasting compounds, concentrated in seeds, found in brassica-family
plants. Interfere with thyroid function, cause liver/kidney lesions, reduce mineral
uptake. Most serious effect: inhibited iodine uptake, reducing thyroxine production and
causing goiters.

GRASS TETANY (aka grass staggers / wheat pasture poisoning)
A metabolic disorder from low blood magnesium. Mainly affects OLDER LACTATING COWS
grazing succulent, immature grass. Symptoms: uncoordinated gait (staggers),
convulsion, coma, death.
Prevention: supplement magnesium via mineral or high-magnesium feed when grass is
lush; graze the highest-risk pastures with steers, heifers, and dry cows instead
(less susceptible classes); increasing legumes to ~30% of pasture composition can also
reduce risk.

NITRATE TOXICITY
Plant nitrate converts to nitrite in the rumen, which binds hemoglobin to form
methemoglobin - reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. Symptoms: grayish/
brownish discoloration of nonpigmented skin and mucous membranes (mouth, nose, eyes,
vulva), blood color shift from bright red to chocolate brown, staggering gait, rapid
pulse, labored breathing, frequent urination, collapse, coma, death. Pregnant animals
may abort.
Risk factors: heavy nitrogen fertilization of cool-season grasses; nitrate can
accumulate to dangerous levels on cool cloudy days, during drought, or under any
condition that slows grass growth/metabolism.
Treatment: intravenous methylene blue if diagnosed early - but since this isn't always
readily available, PREVENTION is the priority: always test forage before feeding when
nitrate risk is suspected.

POLIOENCEPHALOMALACIA (PEM)
High dietary sulfur creates hydrogen sulfide gas in the rumen, leading to brain lesions.
Symptoms: muscle incoordination, circling, stupor, blindness, facial tremors,
recumbency, convulsions, death.
Sulfur guidelines: diets with <15% forage: max tolerable sulfur 0.30%; diets with >40%
forage: max tolerable sulfur 0.50%.
Treatment (supportive): IV thiamine injections and dexamethasone to reduce cerebral
edema.

PRUSSIC ACID POISONING (Hydrocyanic acid / HCN)
Occurs rapidly on high-risk forages, can cause sudden death. HCN deprives cells of
oxygen, causing asphyxiation. Symptoms: staggering, gasping, trembling, convulsions,
respiratory failure, blue-tinged mucous membranes. Diagnosed post-mortem by bright
cherry-red blood.
Treatment: IV sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate (veterinarian administered).
Ruminants (cattle, sheep) are more susceptible than non-ruminants; horses and pigs may
be unaffected.
Key risk windows: new/young regrowth, right after a drought breaks, and IMMEDIATELY
FOLLOWING A FREEZE are all high-HCN periods. Wait 10 days after a killing frost before
grazing/feeding high-risk species. Baled hay is NOT a prussic acid risk since HCN
evaporates during curing/drying (but nitrate risk remains in hay - see below).

SWEETCLOVER POISONING
Coumarin in sweetclover converts to dicoumarin when the plant is SPOILED or damaged
(moldy). Dicoumarin interferes with vitamin K metabolism and blood clotting, causing
hemorrhaging. Occurs mainly from feeding MOLDY sweetclover hay - rarely from grazing
live sweetclover.
Prevention: plant low-coumarin clover varieties. Treatment: IV vitamin K or whole
blood.

SPECIFIC HIGH-RISK PLANTS

HAIRY VETCH
A nitrogen-fixing cover crop NOT recommended as a stock/forage crop due to toxicity in
cattle and horses. Causes an allergic-type reaction: subcutaneous swelling,
photosensitization, hair matting, skin sloughing, oral ulcers, cough, alopecia,
weakness, appetite loss, diarrhea, decreased milk production, sporadic abortion,
red-tinged urine, death. Mortality rate in affected animals: 50-100%, usually from
kidney failure. Risk exists at ANY growth stage.
Highest-risk animals: black-pigmented-skin cattle/horses (Angus, Angus cross,
Holstein, black horses) - though Hereford cattle can also be affected. Poisoning
appears linked to herd genetics (deaths cluster within specific herds), but no genetic
test currently exists to predict individual sensitivity.

LUPIN
Good protein/energy source, but ONLY 4 species are considered nontoxic: narrowflower
lupine, white lupine, European yellow lupine, tarwi. SIX species are particularly
toxic to cattle and sheep: silky, tailcup, velvet, silvery, summer, and sulfur lupine -
these can kill sheep and cause birth defects (cleft palates, crooked legs, malformed
spines) in calves from pregnant cows that consumed them. Species identification
matters critically before grazing any lupin stand.

AMARANTH
Related to pigweed. Grain-production species (love-lies-bleeding, red amaranth,
Prince-of-Wales feather) are marketed for food/cereal use. But several "true weed"
amaranth species are hard to control and problematic: spiny amaranth/pigweed, redroot
pigweed, and Palmer amaranth (consistently HIGH in nitrate and potentially toxic to
cattle). Species-level identification matters here too.

BRASSICAS (kale, rapeseed, swede, turnip, canola, mustard)
High-quality, high-protein feed - aboveground parts run 20-25% crude protein with
60-80% digestibility; roots run 10-14% CP, 80-85% digestible. Ready to graze ~75 days
after planting; regrowth possible if not overgrazed; palatability increases after a
freeze.
Cautions: high moisture, low fiber - offer other dry feeds to maintain rumen function.
Low in copper, manganese, zinc - supplement with a properly balanced mineral,
especially for breeding animals.
Improper-grazing risks: polioencephalomalacia, hemolytic anemia (mainly a kale issue),
pulmonary emphysema, nitrate poisoning, bloat (especially with canola), and
glucosinolate-related metabolic issues. Sheep may show photosensitivity.
Brassicas should comprise NO MORE than 75% of total diet. Offer an iodized mineral
pack to counter glucosinolate effects on iodine uptake. Test for nitrate before
turnout. Introduce animals to a brassica diet gradually over 5-7 days; never turn out
hungry animals - fill them on hay first.
Canola specifically: high in sulfur, raising PEM risk - test sulfur levels in both
plant and water source; provide trace mineralized salt/mineral supplement.

FLAX
Grazing NOT recommended due to prussic acid risk, especially green flax straw right
after a freeze. However, HARVESTED flax SEED is a good high-protein feed (35% CP,
unique fatty acid profile good for horses). Flax straw after seed harvest is
high-cellulose/high-lignin = poor-quality forage regardless of toxicity concerns, so
it's not recommended for grazing OR haying.

SMALL GRAINS (barley, oats, rye, ryegrass, wheat, triticale)
Rapidly growing lush grasses risk grass tetany (more common in spring, but can occur
with cool-season fall/winter growth too). High-protein grasses may contribute to
bloat.
Management: magnesium supplementation (8-12% magnesium free-choice mineral) started
BEFORE turnout, ensuring palatability for adequate intake; increasing legumes to ~30%
of pasture may reduce tetany risk. Nitrate risk increases with heavy nitrogen
fertilization - watch cool, cloudy, drought, or slow-growth conditions.

LEGUMES (general)
Sweetclover, yellow clover, white clover, and low-coumarin varieties (red clover,
Banat sweetclover) do NOT cause sweetclover poisoning when GRAZED live - the risk is
specifically from moldy HAY. Avoid feeding moldy sweetclover hay within 2 weeks of
calving (abortion risk).
Bloat risk: forage under 50% clover is less problematic. Never give hungry animals
access to lush clover stands; provide dry hay first to reduce bloat risk. Clovers pose
LESS bloat risk than alfalfa. Annual lespedeza, birdsfoot trefoil, and sainfoin are NOT
known to cause bloat - though certain birdsfoot trefoil species may carry prussic acid.
Cattle can safely graze lablab, cowpea, sunhemp, mungbean, soybean (low bloat risk,
though sheep are less tolerant - a small percentage of crossbred sheep may show
photosensitivity from cowpea). Sheep grazing rain-damaged mature soybean risk
lupinosis-like symptoms from the fungus Phomopsis - can cause acute liver atrophy and
death.

SORGHUM, SUDANGRASS, MILLETS, AND CORN
All sorghum/sudangrass types carry prussic acid (HCN) risk for grazing cattle - risk
level scales: sudangrass LOWEST (rarely kills), sorghum-sudangrass hybrids
INTERMEDIATE, straight sorghum HIGHEST/most toxic.
Corn does NOT carry prussic acid risk, but cattle are at greater risk than sheep for
prussic acid poisoning generally, and sorghums/corn both carry nitrate toxicity risk.
Sorghum grazing protocol: graze only once plants reach 18"+ height (HCN is highest
when young/growing, right after a drought breaks, during regrowth, and immediately
after a freeze). Use rotational grazing in summer; don't graze any single paddock's
regrowth for more than 5 days. Wait 10 days after a killing frost before grazing or
feeding sorghum, and delay grazing sorghum STALKS specifically until after a killing
freeze. Baled/cured sorghum hay is NOT a prussic acid risk (HCN evaporates during
curing) - but nitrate risk remains in the hay itself.
Sorghum hay nitrate management: test before harvest; raise cutting height, leaving the
bottom third of the stalk in the field (where nitrate concentration is highest); delay
harvest if test results are high, to let the plant metabolize more nitrate.
Millet (pearl or foxtail): nitrate risk possible, but NOT prussic acid risk - these
grasses are considered safe/recommended specifically for horses on that basis.
Corn grazing: Mississippi research found steer gains of 1.9-2.0 lbs/day grazing dried
corn after ear development. Cattle graze SELECTIVELY (leaves, tops, ears, then stalks
last) - risking acidosis/founder from concentrated ear/grain consumption. Management:
strip-graze corn, allowing only 2-3 days of access per paddock, to prevent
over-selection and maintain a stable rumen microbial environment; this also improves
overall forage utilization even when cattle end up only grazing stalks.

QUICK-REFERENCE TABLE HIGHLIGHTS (selected entries; C=class, W=warm/H=high-water-use
etc. omitted for brevity - flagging toxicity notes only)

- Amaranth: some species OK, some poisonous
- Beet (bulb): choking risk, all livestock species
- Brassica hybrid / Kale / Radish / Rapeseed / Turnip: nitrate, polioencephalomalacia,
  anemia, emphysema - all cattle
- Buckwheat: photosensitive dermatitis - horses specifically
- Canola: nitrate toxicity, bloat, polioencephalomalacia - all cattle
- Carrot (root): scouring risk in cattle
- Flax: prussic acid (green flax specifically); seeds are safe
- Mustard: glucosinolate toxicity - all livestock species
- Sunflower: nitrate risk
- Barley/Cereal rye/Oats/Ryegrass/Triticale/Wheat: grass tetany, bloat - all cattle,
  especially lactating cows
- Corn: nitrate, acidosis, founder - all cattle
- Forage sorghum/Foxtail millet/Grain sorghum/Proso millet: nitrate + prussic acid -
  cattle and sheep
- Sorghum-Sudan grass: prussic acid - cattle and sheep
- Sudan grass: minimal prussic acid chance - cattle and sheep
- Alfalfa/Field Pea/Guar/Red clover: bloat risk - all cattle
- Cowpea: minimal bloat chance, photosensitivity possible in sheep
- Forage lespedeza: can cause milk decrease in lactating dairy cows after bloom
- Hairy vetch: poisonous/allergy risk - cattle and horses (NOT recommended as feed)
- Lupin: some species OK, some poisonous - cattle and sheep
- Sainfoin: minimal bloat chance, but potential lupinosis risk in sheep
- Sweetclover / White clover: bloat + "sweet clover poisoning" + vitamin K
  interference - all cattle

BOTTOM-LINE PRACTICAL GUIDANCE

These recommendations are general - always consult a veterinarian before grazing or
feeding any of the plants discussed, and consult an area livestock extension
specialist to select forage crops genuinely suitable for a specific operation. Testing
forage for nitrate before turnout is one of the single highest-value, lowest-cost risk
mitigations available across nearly every crop discussed in this guide.
