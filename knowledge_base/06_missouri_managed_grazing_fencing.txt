WHY MANAGED GRAZING MATTERS FOR WATER QUALITY

Intensively managed grazing can increase productivity/profits while improving water
quality. Grazing systems management addresses this by: optimizing plant growth (raindrop
interception, root mass binding soil), and reducing runoff/leaching of nutrients, manure,
and pesticides. Managed grazing distributes manure more uniformly, improving both
downstream water quality and on-farm water quality (poor water quality can weaken animal
immune systems, hurt weight gains, reproduction, and milk production; algae blooms in
nutrient-rich ponds can be toxic to livestock).

THREE TYPES OF PASTURE SYSTEMS

1. CONTINUOUS GRAZING
   Advantages: lower setup costs (water/fence), less management required, animals can
   select preferred plants.
   Disadvantages: less beef produced per acre; animals use only 30-35% of available
   grass; less desirable plants dominate over time; difficult to maintain legumes; poor
   manure distribution.

2. ROTATIONAL GRAZING (2-7 pastures)
   Advantages: grazing matched to plant growth, rest/regrowth periods, increased forage
   and animal production, some fields can be set aside for haying/stockpiling.
   Disadvantages: increased fencing/water costs, more management/labor, still difficulty
   maintaining legumes, manure distribution still can be concentrated.

3. MANAGEMENT-INTENSIVE GRAZING / MiG (8+ pastures, aka "cell grazing" or
   "short-duration grazing")
   Advantages: increases forage production AND utilization (up to 70% of available
   forage used, vs 30-35% continuous), promotes forage diversity, better animal and
   forage control, fewer weeds, better nutrient cycling, optimizes beef produced per acre.
   Disadvantages: highest initial fencing/water costs, highest management/labor demand.

MANAGEMENT-INTENSIVE GRAZING (MiG) GOAL

Use the best part of ALL plants, not just the most palatable ones. Relatively long rest
periods follow SHORT grazing periods. Grazing periods and move dates are based on degree
of forage utilization, not a preset calendar.

LAYOUT AND DESIGN PRINCIPLES

- Cattle should have water access within 700-900 feet, ideally IN the paddock rather
  than accessed via alleys - alleys cause 15-20% of manure to be dropped off-pasture,
  and cattle drink 15% more water when it's directly in the paddock.
- Paddocks should be as square as possible (minimizes fencing needed per acre, reduces
  distance to water). Rectangular paddocks should not exceed 4x length-to-width ratio.
- Keep soil variation within a single paddock as low as possible - separate bottomland
  from slopes from hilltops (summits) into different paddocks since forage species
  suited to each differ (e.g., ladino clover/reed canary grass on bottomland; birdsfoot
  trefoil/tall fescue on eroded slopes; orchardgrass/alfalfa on productive summits).
- Southwest slopes warm up earlier in spring; northeast slopes hold more moisture and
  stay cooler in summer - cattle may concentrate manure on northeast slopes in summer
  by choosing to lie there.
- On terraced fields, locate fences at the terrace crest; avoid running fences downslope
  across terraces. Straight fences are cheaper and easier to build.
- Aerial photos (from local NRCS/FSA office) and soil survey maps are valuable for
  paddock layout planning.
- Lanes connecting buildings to fields should be located in the driest areas (terrace
  or natural ridge) to avoid gully development from repeated use.

NUMBER OF PADDOCKS

Ideal system moves animals to a fresh paddock DAILY. Benefits: minimal feed waste,
consistent forage quality daily, reduced parasite infestation, rapid/uniform grazing.
To achieve a 3-4 day grazing period (a reasonable target), a system needs a MINIMUM of
8-12 pastures to allow adequate rest.

The carbon-hydrogen-oxygen (CHO) replenishment cycle in forages typically takes 20-40
days. A 20-day rest may suffice under good growing conditions (spring); midsummer
cool-season forage may need 40+ days rest. Fewer paddocks are needed at certain times of
year, or the grazing period can be shortened to compensate. Paddocks not needed for
grazing can be cut for hay/silage.

PADDOCK SIZE FORMULA

Cell size (acres) = (Daily Intake x number of animals x days in cell) /
(yield in lbs/acre x utilization rate as decimal)

STOCKING RATE / GRAZING SYSTEM CALCULATION WALKTHROUGH (worked example)

Rule of thumb: grazing animals consume 3 lbs of dry matter (DM) per 100 lbs of body
weight per day. 1 Animal Unit (AU) = 1,000 lbs live weight, consuming 30 lbs DM/day.

Example: 20 cow/calf pairs averaging 1,250 lbs/pair on 100 acres.
Step 1: (20 pairs x 1,250 lbs) / 1,000 lbs per AU = 25 AU
        25 AU x 30 lbs DM/day-AU = 750 lbs DM needed per day
Step 2: Cool-season grass yielding 2.5 tons/acre/year = ~710 lbs DM/acre per rest period
        (using 20-day spring rest / 40-day summer-fall rest as basis)
Step 3: Decide rotation frequency - dairy/stocker for max production: graze 0.5-1 day;
        beef cow herds: graze less than 5 days, target 3 days or less.
Step 4 (using 3-day grazing rotation as example):
   - 710 lbs DM/acre x 100 acres = 71,000 total lbs DM available per rest period
   - Number of paddocks = (30 days rest / 3 grazing days) + 1 = 11 paddocks
   - 71,000 lbs / 11 paddocks = 6,455 lbs DM available per paddock per rest period
   - 6,455 lbs / 3 grazing days = 2,152 lbs DM available per day
   - 2,152 lbs / 30 lbs DM/day-AU = 71.7 AU stocking rate supportable
   - At 1.25 AU per cow/calf pair (1,250 lb pairs): 71.7 / 1.25 = 57 cow/calf pairs
     the 100 acres can carry
   - 100 acres / 11 paddocks = 9.1 acres per paddock

FENCING SYSTEMS

FIXED FENCING: Interior divider/lane fences typically use one or more 12.5-gauge
high-tensile electrified wires. Two "hot" wires usually suffice for all cattle sizes in
lanes/paddocks (not lots/corrals). Small calves can often "graze ahead" of cows
restrained by a single hot wire. On shallow/rocky soils with poor grounding, a two-wire
fence (one hot, one grounded) may be needed for consistent control.
- Advantages: minimum daily labor, low maintenance, low cost per acre on LARGE
  installations.
- Disadvantages: high cost per acre on small installations, limited flexibility.

PORTABLE/FLEXIBLE FENCING: Electrified tape or polywire on a reel, supported on
lightweight step-in plastic posts. Tape is more visible to cattle and deer but has
shorter lifespan than polywire (flutters in wind). Often used to subdivide large
fixed-fence areas into daily-move paddocks, powered off a fixed electrified boundary
fence.
- Advantages: high flexibility, low cost per acre on small installations.
- Disadvantages: more labor, higher maintenance.

ELECTRIC FENCE ENERGIZERS (CHARGERS)

Power sources: 6V/12V battery, solar, or mains (120/240V AC). Mains power is preferred
when available (more reliable output, no battery costs/hassle). Use only UL-approved or
U.S. Bureau of Standards-approved chargers - NEVER use "homemade" chargers, as they can
kill people and animals.

Solar chargers: battery + solar panel. Rule of thumb: ~7-10 watts of solar panel per
joule of charger output, depending on sunlight/panel orientation. A 10-watt panel costs
roughly $100-300 (at time of publication).

Two charger types:
- High-impedance (older style): "on" time 0.1 sec or less, 45-60 pulses/min, up to
  30,000V, ~25 milliamp current. Works well on clean fences but effectively "dies" if
  shorted by green vegetation.
- Low-impedance ("New Zealand-type"): lower voltage (2,000-6,000V) but much higher
  amperage (up to 30-40A). Pulses ~every second for ~0.0003 sec. Maintains charge past
  a partial short (e.g., grass touching the wire) and won't start grass fires. Pulse
  rate is adjustable on some units (higher rate like 65 ppm for training/deer-prone
  fences, lower like 45 ppm for trained animals to conserve battery).

Guard voltage (voltage present at any point an animal may contact): minimum ~2,000V
usually sufficient for cattle; up to 4,000V may be needed in very dry conditions or for
well-insulated animals like sheep.

Charger sizing rule of thumb: roughly 1 mile of fence per joule of charger rating (up to
6 miles/joule claimed by manufacturers under ideal conditions, but real-world grass/weed
load and insulator quality reduce this). Costs (at time of writing) ranged ~$100 for a
1-joule battery charger to ~$750 for a 240V/28-joule charger. Buy chargers with excess
capacity for future expansion, or use multiple chargers on separate zones (never connect
two chargers to one zone).

GROUNDING

Most electric fences depend on the ground to complete the circuit back through the
animal to the charger - low resistance to ground is critical (preferably 10-25 ohms).
Rule of thumb: 3 feet of ground rod per joule of energizer output. Typically 3-4 ground
rods spaced 10+ feet apart, connected with continuous 12.5-gauge or larger wire. Locate
ground rods in consistently moist earth if possible (e.g., near a building's roof drip
line, north side). Keep ground rods at least 30 feet from other grounds and underground
metal pipes. In very dry/sandy/gravelly soils (low conductivity), an alternating
hot-wire/ground-wire configuration lets animals complete the circuit even when soil
conductivity is poor.

LIGHTNING PROTECTION

Mains-powered chargers should use a surge protector. An induction coil (8-10 turns of
insulated wire, 10-12" diameter coil, at least 20 feet from the charger) acts as a
lightning "choke." Lightning diverters at the lead-out connection and at high points
along the fence route surges to ground - each diverter needs its own solid ground
connection.

HIGH-TENSILE WIRE SPECS

12.5-gauge smooth high-tensile wire (180,000-200,000 psi strength) is standard for
permanent fencing. Look for Class 3 galvanizing (3x thicker than ordinary barbed wire's
Class 1). Unrolled with a "spinning jenny" reel; spliced with a figure-eight knot or
(better) crimped sleeves.

WIRE SPACING GUIDE (from a 1-wire fence at about 2/3 animal shoulder height)

- Cows only: 1 wire, 26-32 inches above ground
- Cows and calves: 2 wires, 18-24" and 24-36"
- Hard-to-hold cattle: 3 wires, 11-18", 23-30" (this middle wire can be grounded in
  very dry/sandy/gravelly conditions), and 34-44"

WIRE TIGHTENERS

Each high-tensile wire needs an in-line tightener every 1/4 mile of straight run to
adjust tension (reel + locking mechanism). Tension springs are used on short stretches
and around high-pressure areas (gates, lots).

POLYWIRE AND POLYTAPE (for temporary/strip grazing)

Polywire: looks like colorful polyethylene twine laced with 3, 6, or 9 strands of wire.
6-9 strand stainless steel is most common for longer runs (less voltage drop). Due to
resistance, best kept under 2,000 feet of run. Spliced with a simple knot.

Polytape: more visible to animals than polywire, but knots don't always make good
electrical contact - use commercial splice buckles or a lock washer as a connector.

Reel capacities: polytape 300-1,320 feet; polywire 660-2,640 feet. Geared reels (3:1
ratio) speed up rewinding.

POSTS AND INSULATORS

Plastic step-in posts with molded-on wire/tape clips (highest clip ~3 ft up) are common
for strip grazing - no insulators needed. Fiberglass sucker rods (3/8" to 5/8"+) also
work without insulators, using wire spring clips.

For permanent fencing: wood or steel line posts spaced up to 100 feet apart on level
ground; 40-60 feet more common on rolling terrain. Corners/ends need 8-foot wood posts
(5-6"+ diameter tops), set 4 feet deep with H-bracing (floating braces are cheaper/
easier for multiwire fences, especially in rocky soil).

Use only UV-stabilized high-density polyethylene or polypropylene insulators on
permanent fences. Minimize steel post use to avoid shorts from cracked/degraded
insulators. For double-hole insulators, ensure wires cross over each other so the
insulator is loaded in COMPRESSION, not tension (tension loading can pull it apart).

GATES

Electrified gates can pass current by going overhead (high enough to clear
livestock/equipment) or by burying insulated wire underground (20,000V-rated
direct-burial wire is available; running it inside plastic pipe reduces short-circuit
risk). Gates should be non-electrified when open.

Electrified floodgates: droppers attached to the wire spaced 6" above normal water
level, with floodgate controllers available to limit current flow when flooded.

TRAINING ANIMALS TO ELECTRIC FENCE

Electric fence is a PSYCHOLOGICAL barrier, not a physical one - animals must be trained
to respect it. Train with a hot wire inside a permanent fence/corral so animals can't
escape if they push through. Include an open gate during training so animals learn some
areas are "safe" to pass through, since they'll be moving through gates regularly in a
managed grazing system.
