SHEET 1: INTRODUCTION

WHAT IS PASTURELAND?
Land supporting introduced or domestic native forage species, used primarily for
livestock production, receiving periodic renovation/cultural treatment (tillage,
fertilizer, mowing, weed control). Permanent pastureland = no intent to rotate to
other crops. Some pastureland is irrigated; some carries trees as a secondary
silvicultural enterprise; some is open savanna or partially cleared forest where
introduced forage has naturalized and displaced most native herbaceous plants.

WHAT IS PASTURELAND CONDITION?
The status of the plant community and soil relative to the pasture's highest possible
condition under "ideal" management. The land user selects the desired plant community
based on adaptability to existing soil/climate, livestock production goals, and
livestock forage preferences.
Under ideal management, grazing pressure and inputs are managed to keep the desired
plant community reasonably stable at desired species proportions. Over time, without
intervention, permanent pastures naturalize - unintended species invade, shifting
composition away from the land user's goals, often toward lower forage quality/
quantity and reduced responsiveness to inputs.

WHAT IS SOIL / SOIL QUALITY?
Soil is a dynamic resource: mineral particles (sand/silt/clay), organic matter, air,
water, and living organisms, with biological/chemical/physical properties that shift
in response to management.
Soil quality = the capacity of a specific soil to function within natural/managed
ecosystem boundaries, sustain plant/animal productivity, maintain/enhance water and
air quality, and support human health/habitation.

WHAT DOES SOIL QUALITY AFFECT ON PASTURELAND?
Plant production, reproduction, and mortality; erosion; vegetation composition; water
availability and quality; wildlife habitat; carbon sequestration; livestock health and
production.

HOW ARE SOIL QUALITY AND PASTURE CONDITION RELATED?
They're interdependent. Example causal chain given: increased surface compaction from
excessive/untimely livestock traffic → decreased water infiltration capacity → less
water available to plants → declining plant production → some species disappear while
less-desirable species increase → in severe/prolonged cases, bare areas appear.
The reverse chain also holds: vigorous, rapidly-growing plants increase organic matter
inputs → improved water infiltration/availability → reduced erosion potential via
vegetative cover (less raindrop impact, less soil movement).
Important caveat: some shifts are IRREVERSIBLE (e.g. accelerated erosion changing the
soil profile itself), while others are recoverable through changed grazing management.

WHY DOES SOIL QUALITY MATTER PRACTICALLY?
Changes in soil quality from agronomic/grazing management affect: how much rainfall/
snowmelt is actually available for plant growth; surface runoff and erosion potential;
nutrient availability for plant growth; seed germination, seedling establishment,
vegetative reproduction, root growth, forage production; and the soil's ability to
filter/protect water and air.

SHEET 2: INDICATORS FOR ASSESSMENT AND MONITORING

WHAT ARE INDICATORS?
Key soil or plant community characteristics that are measurable and sensitive to
changes in management or environment. Environmental factors (e.g. time since last
rainfall) can affect some indicators independent of management and must be considered
when interpreting results.

CATEGORIES OF INDICATORS USED ON PASTURELAND

Soil properties: physical (bulk density, soil strength - reflect root growth/seedling
emergence/infiltration limits), chemical (nutrients, organic matter, cation-exchange
capacity, base saturation, pH - directly affect plant production and cover ability),
and biological (earthworms and other organisms - some beneficial, some harmful like
insect larvae/nematodes that can damage forage stands or infect livestock). Organic
matter and aggregate stability reflect a COMBINATION of all three property types.

Soil surface features: pedestals, exposed roots, rills, gullies, wind scours,
terracettes, penetration resistance, stoniness, debris dams/windrows after storms -
these reflect runoff and erosion processes, usually assessed qualitatively.

Vegetative features: plant cover, desirable species presence, plant residue, plant
vigor, species diversity - reflect the current plant community's makeup and cycling.

Management factors: uniformity of plant removal, excessive-use areas, species
adaptation to site, plant stress from insects/disease, current soil fertility/pH
status - these are often the PRIMARY CAUSES behind changes in soil quality, making
them the most actionable category.

Environmental conditions: level of plant physiological stress from weather/site
conditions at time of assessment - a confounding factor to account for, not a
management lever.

Spatial patterns and variability: landscape position, erosion, deposition affect
water/nutrient distribution and cycling across a pasture. As distribution of organic
matter and animal waste becomes LESS uniform (from poor grazing/traffic distribution),
resource availability decreases in some patches and increases in others -
inconsistency itself becomes a problem, not just overall averages.

ASSESSMENT PRACTICALITIES

Start any pasture assessment with the land user's DESIRED plant community, production
goals, and environmental goals as the reference standard - not some generic ideal.
NRCS forage suitability group reports and local university extension forage references
should supplement this.

Timing matters: some soil properties are relatively insensitive to seasonal change
(e.g. total organic matter), while others (e.g. soil compaction, measured by
penetration resistance) vary with recent soil moisture/temperature - for these, always
maintain a livestock-traffic-free REFERENCE SITE (like a fence line) for comparison.

Common assessment objectives: selecting monitoring sites (riparian zones, heavily-used
areas, noxious weed areas), gathering inventory data for decisions (soil tests),
identifying at-risk areas (streambanks, shorelines, access lanes), and targeting
management inputs (stocking, fertilizer, water development, overseeding).

Careful site selection matters: a pasture on varied landscape is best evaluated by
sampling each representative landscape position/soil type separately, or by targeting
only the positions with obvious problems. Note off-site influences (crop fields,
woodlots, hedgerows, farmsteads, roads, past disturbances) as context for
interpretation - e.g. a sparse stand in a recently row-crop-converted area has an
obvious explanatory history.

MONITORING PRACTICALITIES

Monitoring = orderly collection/analysis/interpretation of quantitative data over time
at PERMANENTLY MARKED locations, ideally with baseline data to establish trend.
Designed so different observers can measure consistently.

Common monitoring objectives: evaluating/documenting progress toward management
goals, detecting early-warning changes before they become bigger problems, and
determining overall trend (maintaining/declining/recovering).

Site selection depends on objective: representative sites for progress/trend tracking;
MOST VULNERABLE sites if the goal is catching problems early enough to modify
management before they occur.

Important expectation-setting: most degraded soil quality conditions CAN be reversed
or even improved beyond the previous native condition - but for some properties (like
organic matter content), it may take SEVERAL YEARS TO DECADES to reach a new
equilibrium level for the specific site. Set monitoring frequency and expectations
accordingly - don't expect fast wins on slow-moving indicators like total soil organic
matter, even when management is doing everything right.
