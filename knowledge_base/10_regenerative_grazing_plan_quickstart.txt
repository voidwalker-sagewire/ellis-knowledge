CORE PHILOSOPHY

Regenerative grazing management is OUTCOME-focused, not practice-specific. It is NOT
the same thing as simply practicing daily rotational movement - simply moving animals
daily through a rotation is NOT automatically "regenerative grazing." What matters is
managing toward specific desired outcomes: soil health, forage biodiversity, animal
performance, profitability, etc.

Direct quote: "Regenerative grazing is outcome-focused, not practice-specific... it
should be clear that simply practicing daily movement of animals through a rotational
grazing system is NOT regenerative grazing."

THE 8-STEP PROCESS

STEP 1 - IDENTIFY GOALS
Make goals specific to your operation's context. Write them down and check every
management decision against them. Common regenerative goals: increasing organic
matter/sequestering carbon, increasing plant/animal biodiversity, improving
profitability by a specific amount. No universal right answer - the important part is
specificity and active monitoring against the written goals.

STEP 2 - CREATE A PASTURE INVENTORY
List every pasture (one per row on a grazing chart), including crop fields if animals
ever graze residues/cover crops. Temporary subdivisions from portable fencing (poly
wire strips) don't need separate listing.
Create a corresponding map showing water points, fences, features (existing FSA maps,
apps like MaiaGrazing/PastureMap/AgriWebb, or Google Earth work).
Record grazeable acres per pasture.
Note forage species COMPOSITION per pasture (e.g. "cool-season," "warm-season," or a
mix like "50% cool-season, 50% warm-season") - this drives recovery periods, residue
height targets, and seasonal growth curve expectations.
Estimate forage availability per pasture: methods include a grazing stick (canopy
height), clip-and-weigh within a quadrat, or experienced visual estimation of one
cow/animal-unit's daily consumption. Large pastures often have a mix of highly and
poorly productive zones - estimate each separately and sum for a realistic total.
Determine the optimal RECOVERY PERIOD range for each pasture after grazing. Too short
= negative impact; too long can also be undesirable. Recovery period is ultimately
governed by (1) length of grazing events on OTHER pastures and (2) total number of
paddocks in the operation. Record recovery period by month on the grazing chart for
planning.

STEP 3 - CALCULATE CARRYING CAPACITY (Animal Unit Days per Acre / ADA)
Express carrying capacity as total days of grazing a pasture will produce, standardized
to a "Standard Animal Unit" (1,000 lb cow). Convert estimated forage availability per
pasture into ADA and record on the chart.

STEP 4 - MAKE AN ANIMAL INVENTORY
List each species/class of grazing livestock and head count present on day one of the
plan. Recognize that AU numbers will shift through the year (sales, weight changes in
growing animals) - update monthly on the chart.
Example AU calculation: 100 stocker calves at 600 lbs average, using an Animal Unit
Equivalent (AUE) of 0.6 per calf = 100 x 0.6 = 60 AU total.

STEP 5 - MARK IMPORTANT DATES
Mark holidays, anniversaries, vacations, and any date no one will be available to move
animals - these constrain the plan. Also mark:
- Use limitations/resource concerns (e.g. avoiding mud-prone bottom pastures in wet
  seasons, avoiding hunting-disturbance areas seasonally)
- Livestock production-stage windows (calving, breeding seasons - nutritional
  requirements peak here, so matching forage resources to these windows matters most)
- Key management events needing corral/handling-facility access (vaccinations,
  branding, pregnancy diagnosis, AI, shipping)

STEP 6 - PLAN GRAZING MOVEMENTS
Record planned turn-in and removal dates per pasture, per herd, on the chart (one box
= one day). When the herd moves, jump to the next pasture's row.
Counterintuitive but useful technique: plan BACKWARDS - identify calendar dates
animals need to be somewhere specific (e.g. corrals for vaccination) FIRST, then work
out how to route the herd there through the pasture sequence.
Formula: Average grazing period = Recovery Period / (Number of Pastures - 1)
IMPORTANT: intentionally "mix it up" year to year. Repeating the same pasture sequence
at the same time every year has downsides (reduced forage species diversity over
time from repeated same-season pressure). Avoid permanently assigning certain pastures
to certain seasonal roles (e.g. always "the calving pasture") when practical.

STEP 7 - MONITOR, MANAGE ADAPTIVELY, RE-PLAN
Record ACTUAL grazing event dates on the chart under the planned lines (use a
different color to compare plan vs. actual). Compare actual forage
productivity/utilization against original estimates - lagging growth may signal a
destocking decision is needed.
Track rainfall/snowfall on the chart below the animal movement section - relating
precipitation to forage growth helps predict drought and inform destocking timing.
"Destock dates": establish specific calendar dates ahead of time at which stocking
rate will be reduced in response to drought metrics (e.g. percentage of average annual
rainfall received by a given date, or forage inventory vs. current stocking rate
comparisons at the start of seasonally dry periods). Committing to dates in advance
removes the emotional/reactive difficulty of destocking decisions made in the moment.
At season's end, total the ADA harvested per pasture - this becomes the following
year's baseline for planning.

STEP 8 - ESTABLISH AND MONITOR OTHER METRICS
Soil health/fertility monitoring is strongly encouraged: track fertility, soil
aggregation, water infiltration ability, water holding capacity - establish baselines
to measure change over time.
Other potential metrics: pounds of calf weaned per acre, profitability of the grazing
enterprise and overall operation, quality-of-life metrics for family/employees,
wildlife or habitat metrics.
Direct quote: "Establishing baselines and monitoring progress towards your established
outcomes and goals is what helps make your operation regenerative."

KEY TAKEAWAY

The grazing chart itself (a spreadsheet or paper chart with pastures as rows and
months/days as columns) is the central planning tool - it holds the pasture inventory,
ADA figures, animal inventory, important dates, planned AND actual grazing events, and
rainfall data all in one place, enabling both up-front planning and adaptive
mid-season replanning.
