WHAT IS PASTURE CONDITION SCORING (PCS)?

A systematic way to assess how well a pasture is being managed and resources protected.
A high score = well-managed pasture with plant and animal productivity being sustained
or enhanced. A low score typically means one or more challenges exist: poor plant growth,
weedy species invasion, poor animal performance, visible soil loss, increased runoff,
or impaired water quality in/adjacent to the pasture.

WHEN TO PERFORM PCS

- As a benchmark condition of the pasture
- Early in the growing season before grazing events occur
- At peak forage supply periods
- At low forage supply periods
- At plant stress periods (drought or very wet conditions)
- When conservation practices/management have been fully applied
- Best single timing: just before a pasture is grazed
- For best trend-tracking, evaluate the same pasture at the same time each year

PCS is NOT a replacement for a forage inventory or forage production estimate - it's a
complementary diagnostic tool.

THE 10 INDICATORS (each scored 1-5, lowest to highest condition; summed for overall score)

1. PERCENT DESIRABLE PLANTS
Desirable species: well-adapted to site, readily consumed, persistent, high tonnage/
quality for a significant part of the growing season (e.g. orchardgrass, white clover,
Kentucky bluegrass, big bluestem). Often GRAZED FIRST and closest to the ground in
poorly managed systems - so a declining desirable-species score is itself diagnostic of
overgrazing, since less-palatable species increase as desirables get selectively grazed
out.
Intermediate species: adapted to the site, eaten but provide low production or lose
quality fast (dandelions, wild plantains, barnyard grass, hop clover). These increase
first as desirables decline - an early warning sign.
Undesirable species: rejected by livestock, cause negative effects, or have little forage
value (nimblewill, wild garlic, horsenettle, buttercup). On severely overstocked sites,
undesirables become the ONLY surviving plants.
Scoring: 1 = <20% desirable species by dry weight; 5 = >80% desirable.

2. PERCENT LEGUME
Legumes comprising at least 20% of dry-weight forage meaningfully improve nitrogen
supply and forage quality. Deep-rooted legumes provide grazing during hot, dry
midsummer periods when grasses may struggle.
BLOAT WARNING: bloating legumes (clovers, alfalfa) above 40% of total forage dry
weight make bloat likely in ruminants without preventative measures.
Visual estimation tip: legume leaves sit in the upper part of the plant (denser-looking
canopy) vs. grasses which are denser at the base - visual "wet" percentage typically
needs to be roughly halved to estimate true dry-weight percentage (except red clover
and alfalfa, which read closer to their visual estimate).
Scoring: 1 = <5% legume (or >50% bloating legume); 5 = 31-40% legume with no grass
loss (grass may even be increasing).

3. LIVE PLANT COVER (includes dormant)
Rates how well the pasture's "solar panel" is working - higher live leaf area means
higher photosynthetic activity, better animal intake, better sunlight capture. Bare/open
spots invite weeds, runoff, erosion, and lost production.
Three forage growth stages: Stage 1 (short, immature, high quality but low production),
Stage 2 (greatest live leaf surface area, generally best forage quality), Stage 3
(maturing/dormant vegetation - greatest forage VOLUME but declining photosynthesis
and quality).
Important nuance: letting forage mature longer can be a POSITIVE management move for
building deeper roots and soil organic matter - dormant/stockpiled forage shouldn't
automatically score a 5, but can still score moderately well if other indicators are met.
Best assessment timing on rotational pastures: estimate canopy cover the day BEFORE
livestock entry (represents best-case condition).
Scoring: 1 = <40% live leaf canopy; 5 = >95% live (non-dormant) leaf canopy.

4. PLANT DIVERSITY
Multiple dominant desirable species act as "insurance" against stress - warm-season
grasses can provide quality forage during hot dry summer periods when cool-season
forage goes dormant, for example. Low diversity = high vulnerability to changing
conditions.
Key definitions: a "dominant species" = at least 15% of pasture biomass by dry weight.
A "functional group" = species sharing similar management needs/biology (the four basic
groups for most of the US: cool-season grasses, warm-season grasses, legumes, and other
grazable nonleguminous forbs like brassicas/chicory/dandelion).
Mixed pastures with at least 2 functional groups and 3+ well-represented species are
generally most productive. Higher diversity WITHIN a single functional group doesn't
necessarily boost productivity, and can cause livestock to selectively avoid some
species while overgrazing others.
Scoring rule: desirable species must total more than 50% of biomass to score above a 1.
Any time undesirables outnumber desirables, automatic score of 1.

5. PLANT RESIDUE AND LITTER AS SOIL COVER
Soil cover slows evaporation, stabilizes soil temperature, feeds soil life (carbon
source), deters erosion, aids water infiltration. Litter = uppermost freshly-fallen or
slightly decomposed material; residue = detached material in varying decay states,
subject to constant microbial processing.
Extremely intensively-grazed systems (dairy, beef finishing kept in constant vegetative
stage) often LACK enough residue/litter most of the season - fix by increasing rest
period (allows more trampling of mature forage onto soil surface).
Conversely, EXCESSIVE residue/litter can interfere with new tiller growth and tie up
nitrogen, usually signaling insufficient biological activity - fix by shortening rest
period, adding diversity (especially legumes), and increasing stock density.
General target: minimum ~60% cover with good biological activity, though exact ideal
varies with local biology.
Scoring: 1 = <20% soil cover, excessive/slow-to-break-down cover; 5 = >80% cover with
good biological activity and decomposition.

6. GRAZING UTILIZATION AND SEVERITY
Close/frequent grazing reduces plant vigor, desired species density, and yield;
promotes erosion; affects bite size/intake.
Key distinction: UNIFORM grazing (almost all desirable/intermediate species grazed to
target "stop-grazing" height or slightly higher) only happens with proper management
and smaller allocations. An overgrazed pasture may LOOK uniform (mowed-lawn appearance)　
but this is the LOWEST rating - uniformity alone isn't the goal, uniformity at the
RIGHT height is.
"Zone grazing" = one end heavily grazed, other end lightly/ungrazed - common where
walking distance to water/shade/feeding areas is long, or where topography is abrupt.
Livestock will also typically refuse to graze near recent manure/urine deposits, driving
uneven grazing cycles.
Scoring: 1 = overgrazed throughout; 3 = zone grazing plus uneven grazing; 4 = uniformly
grazed to target height with SOME zone grazing; 5 = grazed evenly throughout, no
overgrazing.

7. LIVESTOCK CONCENTRATION AREAS
Places where livestock linger/return frequently (feeding areas, gates, water, mineral/
salt, shade) - often have reduced vegetative cover, more bare ground, concentrated
waste. Trails to/from these areas can become erosion/sediment/nutrient/pathogen
conduits to nearby water.
Assessed by SIZE of disturbed area AND proximity/connectivity to surface water.
Reference: 1 acre = 208' x 208'; 0.1 acre = 66' x 66'. For fields under 1 acre, use 10%
of the grazing unit's area as the threshold instead of the flat 0.1-acre figure.
Scoring: 1 = concentration areas within 100 ft of water AND over 0.1 acre; 5 = no
concentration areas/trails present at all.

8. SOIL COMPACTION AND SOIL REGENERATIVE FEATURES
Compaction reduces pore space, minimizing water infiltration and increasing bulk
density - directly hurting hydrologic function, nutrient cycling, and root access to
water/nutrients. Increased runoff from compaction can transport sediment/nutrients/
pathogens to surface water.
Evaluation method: dig a hole with a shovel large enough to see the indicator features.
For comparison, dig a second hole in a protected reference area (e.g. a fence line
where grazing occurs but hoof action hasn't compacted soil) on the SAME soil type.
Features to observe: ease of shovel entry, soil structure (platiness/aggregation in
top 12"), rooting depth, root direction (should trend downward through profile), color
contrast (darker = more biotically active upper layer), worms/tunnels/biotic activity.
Primary sub-indicators for scoring: compaction layer, then root characteristics - use
the MORE ADVERSE of these two to set the score. Soil color and soil life are secondary/
discussion indicators.
Scoring: 1 = dense/thick platy layer very distinct, roots dominantly horizontal and
shallow/sparse; 5 = no dense/platy layers, crumbly soil throughout, roots growing
abundantly downward, numerous signs of soil life.

9. PLANT VIGOR
The health/robustness of a plant relative to others of the same species under the same
conditions. Deliberately scored NEAR THE END of the PCS process, since earlier
indicators inform this judgment.
Color is a major vigor indicator: yellowing = drought, insect damage, or prolonged
heavy/continuous grazing. Pale green = low fertility or cool/wet/poor growing
conditions. Dark green spots under dung/urine patches contrasted against pale
surrounding pasture = nitrogen-starved grass elsewhere in the field. Frost damage =
yellow or blue-gray cast. A lush, dark green-to-bluish-green, LODGED appearance can
indicate OVER-fertilization (especially nitrogen) - rate this a 1 if observed, as excess
nitrogen applications can cause nitrate toxicity issues.
Growth rate / recovery after grazing is the other key trait. Ideally: slow growth
periods need LONGER recovery, fast growth periods need SHORTER recovery. Recovery
period is heavily influenced by how severely the pasture was grazed last time - the
more severe (below recommended stop-grazing height), the longer the needed recovery.
Continuously-grazed pastures have little to no recovery time by definition.
Scoring: 1 = no recovery after grazing, pale/yellow/brown or severe stunting; 5 = rapid
recovery, all healthy green forage.

10. EROSION (always rate sheet-and-rill; rate others only if present)
Sheet and Rill: caused by raindrop impact, drip splash onto bare soil, and thin
sheet-flow runoff. Increases as cover decreases. Evidence: small debris dams of plant
residue at obstructions; rills are small parallel incised channels downslope - their
appearance signals SERIOUS soil loss. Includes most irrigation-induced erosion.
Streambank/Shoreline: livestock traffic patterns, frequency/duration/intensity of use,
and channel/bank attractiveness (sunning, dusting, travel lanes, watering, rubbing)
all affect the extent of livestock-caused bank disturbance. Only erosion caused/
influenced by livestock use counts toward this sub-score.
Wind: transport/deposition of soil from heavier windblown particles abrading and
exposing more soil; deposition occurs downwind of obstructions (fence lines, buildings,
vegetation). Extreme cases: soil abrades and smothers vegetation.
Gullies: an ADVANCED stage of water erosion where rill erosion went unaddressed.
Concentrated fast-moving water causes expansion via mass soil caving (sides) and
head-cutting (upslope), creating deep channels.
Scoring rule: rate the overall erosion indicator using the LOWEST (worst) score among
all erosion types present - i.e., the worst problem sets the score, not an average.

OVERALL SCORE INTERPRETATION (sum of all 10 indicator scores, range 10-50)

45-50: No changes in management needed at this time.
35-45: Minor changes would enhance condition - do the most beneficial ones first.
25-35: Improvements would benefit productivity and/or environment.
15-25: Needs immediate management changes; high return on action likely.
10-15: Major effort required in time, management, and expense.

PRACTICAL METHOD NOTES

The "step-point method" is recommended for reducing subjectivity - it can generate
quantitative data for 5 of the 10 indicators directly (percent desirable plants, percent
legume, live plant cover, plant diversity, plant residue). Pacing off distances helps
assess livestock concentration areas objectively, and a simple shovel test handles soil
compaction/regeneration.

Refer to your State or regional desirable/intermediate/undesirable plant list, ideally
broken out by grazing livestock type (cattle, sheep, goats), since desirability
classifications shift by species and geography.
