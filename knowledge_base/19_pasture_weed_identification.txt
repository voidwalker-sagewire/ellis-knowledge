WHY IDENTIFY WEEDS FIRST

A weed is simply "a plant growing where you don't want it to grow." A pure grass stand
is essentially impossible - volunteers always arrive from the seedbank or neighboring
fields. New volunteers can mean MORE/better forage, or losses of quality/yield - the
only way to know which is correct identification. Weeds can also injure or kill
livestock if toxic, reduce hay yield/quality, and interfere with hay drying.

SCOUTING METHOD

Walk fields and scout at least 10-15 sites per field. Identify all plants and
seedlings at each site. Best assessment timing: between seasons, about 1 week after
cutting or grazing. Keep a written record of which weeds appear in which field and
when first noticed - this tracks whether your control program is actually working.
Some weeds take several years to bring under control, so records matter for judging
real progress vs. normal year-to-year variation.

PREVENTION

Weeds spread via: hay bales, plants reaching maturity and seeding, livestock
movement, mowing equipment, wind, water, wildlife. Contaminated pasture seed is
another entry vector - buy CERTIFIED seed to avoid introducing weed seed along with
your forage seed purchase.

FOUR CONTROL METHOD CATEGORIES (used in combination, not alone)

1. CULTURAL CONTROL - increases forage competitiveness so it out-competes weeds.
   Fertilizing to correct soil pH and fertility is foundational - get this right BEFORE
   layering on the other three methods.

2. GRAZING MANAGEMENT - rotational grazing gives desired plants rest/regrowth time
   they need to out-compete weeds, and in rotational systems animals will often
   consume weeds they'd otherwise avoid under continuous grazing (less selective
   avoidance when forage choice is more limited per paddock). Graze/mow at proper
   height - never cut or graze too short, which favors weedy species over desired
   forage.

3. MECHANICAL CONTROL - usually mowing. More effective on BROADLEAF weeds than grass
   weeds. Downsides: fuel cost, limited effect on large/mature weeds, and mowing can
   actually SPREAD seed and encourage more weed growth if timed wrong. Burning (where
   safe/permitted) is another mechanical-adjacent option for some forage types.

4. CHEMICAL CONTROL - herbicides inhibiting plant growth processes. Select based on
   desired forage species, weed species present, cost, and application ease. Best
   postemergence timing is when weeds are young and actively growing:
   - Cool-season weeds: best controlled October-December (also possible Feb-April)
   - Warm-season weeds: best controlled April to mid-July
   Always read and follow label directions, including grazing/haying restriction
   windows - the label is legally binding, not just a suggestion.

KEY PLANT ID VOCABULARY (condensed glossary)

- Broadleaf (dicot): net-veined leaves, often wider than long, can have taproot/
  bulbous/fibrous roots, often showy flowers, may have square or round stems
- Grass/grass-like (monocot): parallel-veined, lance-like leaves
- Sedge: resembles grass but has a distinctly TRIANGULAR/three-sided stem - this is
  the reliable way to distinguish sedges from true grasses
- Annual: germinates, flowers, seeds, and dies within one year
- Biennial: 2-year cycle - forms a basal rosette (roots/leaves) year one, flowers/
  seeds/dies year two
- Perennial: lives longer than two years
- Rosette: circular cluster of leaves at ground level, typical of biennial weeds
  overwintering before their flowering year
- Rhizome: underground creeping stem producing roots/shoots at nodes
- Stolon: ABOVEground creeping stem producing roots/shoots at nodes
- Taproot: single main root growing straight down, with smaller lateral roots

TOXIC/PROBLEM WEEDS - IDENTIFICATION DETAIL

HORSENETTLE (perennial, from rhizomes)
Obvious leaf spines, up to 3 ft tall, star-shaped white-to-violet flowers with yellow
center, berry fruit (green→yellow, wrinkled at maturity). Capable of poisoning
livestock, but consumption is rare in practice BECAUSE of the prickly stems/leaves
naturally deterring grazing.

PIGWEED / AMARANTH (annual, ~60 species)
Red stems, egg-shaped wavy-margined alternate leaves, up to 6.5 ft tall, shallow
often-reddish taproot, small inconspicuous green flowers in dense compact panicles.
CAN BE VERY TOXIC in large quantities due to potential NITRATE accumulation. Spiny
amaranth and redroot pigweed are the two most common types on disturbed soils - spiny
amaranth is distinguished by paired spines at the base of the leaf petiole and central
stem.

POKEWEED (perennial, 1-10 ft)
Alternate pointed crinkled-edge leaves, pink/red stems, greenish-white flowers
developing into dark purple berries. Commonly grows along fence lines.

JOHNSONGRASS (aggressive perennial grass, spreads by rhizome AND seed, up to 8 ft)
Prominent white leaf midvein, pink-to-rusty-red stem base, purplish hairy panicles.
CRITICAL SAFETY NOTE: can be poisonous to livestock after wilting from frost, or new
regrowth following drought or cutting - during hot dry weather, johnsongrass can carry
enough PRUSSIC ACID to kill livestock in any quantity consumed. Can also cause death
from nitrate poisoning. This is a genuinely dangerous pasture grass under the wrong
conditions despite being a grass, not a broadleaf "weed" in the usual sense.

DALLISGRASS (perennial, resembles crabgrass, usable as forage grass)
Identified by hairy spikelets in 4 rows on 3-8 alternating branches. CAUTION: rank
seedheads late in the season can become infected with an ergot-like fungus (turning
gray-to-black and sappy) - cattle eating significant amounts of infected seedheads can
develop "dallisgrass staggers," a serious but not usually fatal illness.

HAIRY VETCH (annual/perennial legume, climbing to 6.5 ft)
White-woolly appearance from long soft hairs, purple-to-blue flowers, shallow taproot
with strong lateral branches. (Cross-reference: separately documented as toxic to
cattle/horses in the toxic plants file - grows best on dry sandy soils.)

WILD GARLIC / WILD ONION (perennial, from bulbs)
Easily confused, but distinguishable by leaf cross-section: wild garlic leaves are
HOLLOW and ROUND; wild onion leaves are FLATTER and more SOLID. Wild garlic persists
later into the growing season than wild onion.

BUTTERCUP (bulbous and hairy species, annual, common pasture weed)
Deeply lobed leaves, yellow 5-petal flowers. Bulbous buttercup shows a distinctive
bulbous swelling at the stem base - a reliable ID feature.

SICKLEPOD (annual, 1-6 ft)
Yellow flowers, long narrow seed pods, multidivided leaves. Leaflets are
PHOTOSENSITIVE - they fold upward at night or on cloudy days (a genuinely useful field
ID trick). Plants AND seeds are potentially toxic.

COMMON LESPEDEZA (annual, mat-forming, usable as forage)
Dark green trifoliate leaves with parallel veins near-right-angle to midvein, pink-
to-purple flowers late summer. A genuinely useful forage species that also commonly
volunteers as a "weed" in unwanted locations - context (is it wanted here or not)
matters more than the plant itself for this one.

NUTSEDGE (yellow and purple - true sedges, not grasses despite the name "nutgrass")
Distinguished by triangular stem cross-section (the sedge tell). Yellow nutsedge:
yellowish-tan seedhead, tubers grow only at rhizome ENDS, chestnut/tan-colored tubers.
Purple nutsedge: red-purple seedhead, tubers form in a CHAIN connected by rhizomes,
hairy charcoal/black-colored tubers. Purple nutsedge is generally harder to control,
so correct species ID matters for treatment planning.

FIELD SANDBUR / SANDSPUR (annual, sandy soils)
Lighter green than most grass weeds, rough leaves. Mainly a physical nuisance/hazard
- green or brown spiny burs cause discomfort to livestock and disperse by clinging to
animals, people, clothing, equipment.

CURLY DOCK (perennial, deep taproot, up to 5 ft)
Basal rosette of wavy-margined leaves, unbranched stem, leaves turn reddish-purple
with age. Gets progressively harder to control as the plant matures and the taproot
develops - earlier intervention is meaningfully easier than later.

DANDELION (3 species: Carolina false, common, cat's ear)
Carolina false dandelion is a winter annual/biennial; common and cat's ear are
perennials. Distinguishing feature: young leaves of common dandelion are hairless,
while cat's ear dandelion leaves are densely hairy with more rounded lobes.

RYEGRASS (perennial vs. Italian/annual)
Both used for winter cover/grazing/hay, both common volunteers. Italian ryegrass has
long clasping auricles and 9-15+ seeds per seedhead with an awn on seed tips;
perennial ryegrass has short non-clasping auricles, typically 7-9 seeds, no awn.

PRACTICAL FRAMEWORK FOR ELLIS TO APPLY

1. When a weed is reported or described, first establish: is this a genuine problem
   weed, a forage species growing somewhere unwanted, or a species with dual
   identity (like common lespedeza or dallisgrass) where context determines whether
   it's wanted?
   2. Check specifically whether the species carries a TOXICITY risk (cross-reference
   the toxic plants file) before recommending any control approach that assumes it's
   "just" a competitive nuisance weed.
3. Recommend starting with cultural control (soil fertility/pH correction) as the
   foundation, then layering grazing management, mechanical, and/or chemical control
   as needed - not chemical control as a first resort.
4. Remind the person that some weed problems take multiple YEARS of consistent
   management to resolve - this sets realistic expectations rather than implying a
   single intervention should show fast results.
